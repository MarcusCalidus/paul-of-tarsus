A level file holds everything that belongs to one chapter and nothing else does: its music and ambience, its artwork, its cast and items, its rooms with their hotspots and dialog, its cutscenes and its prayer hints. `index.html` keeps only a manifest with one line per chapter (`CHAP`: name, file, code salt), and fetches a level with a script tag the first time it is needed, so the game still runs when opened straight from disk. To add a chapter, write its file under `levels/` and add its line to the manifest. (A safe-point code begins with its chapter's number: nothing for Chapter One, one digit for Chapters Two to Nine, two digits from Chapter Ten on. See "Safe-point codes" below.)

Inside a level file:

- **Music and ambience** are plain data. A score names a tempo, a tonic, a mode and a list of tracks (drone, drum pattern, step-sequenced instrument, melody); a bar is 16 steps unless the score says otherwise with `steps` (12, 10 or 14 for bars in threes, fives or sevens), and the patterns, the melody lines, the loop, the drones, the downbeat and the accents follow it; each track lists the moods it plays in, and each room names its mood (`calm`, `tense`, `hope`, or one of the chapter's own, such as `feast` in Chapter Six) and the sounds around it, for example `amb:{waves:.8,gulls:.6}`, and may say how much room it has: `space:1.5` in a synagogue, `space:.5` on an open road (1 if it says nothing, as for scenes). Voices sent with `echo:1` sound in that room, a short synthesized reverberation, and the amount glides when you walk from one room to the next. When the mood changes, in a new room or in the middle of a scene, the tracks of the old mood fade out and those of the new one fade in over a few steps; a drone that joins its mood between strikes is struck at once. Above about 550 Hz the ney, the aulos and the overtones of the plucked strings are played a little softer (`bite()`, some 3 dB by 1200 Hz), so a tune can be moved up an octave without growing shrill. The instruments, drums and ambience voices themselves live in the engine. The plucked strings and the drums are played by hand: each note lands a few milliseconds off the grid, and notes that start a beat are a little louder. Beats are fours unless a track, or the whole score, says how a bar falls, for example `group:[3,3,2]` for a harp that rocks in three, three and two; the held voices (ney, aulos, drone) play exactly as written. The aulos is a pair of pipes that play the same note; a track that says `hold:0` (or `hold:7`) has its second pipe hold the tonic (or the fifth) under the melody instead, breathed again bar by bar, as in Chapter Six's feast. The engine remembers the key of the chapter (`AU.key`: its score's root and scale) even while no score plays, as on the map and at a chapter's end. `note(degree, oct)` gives the MIDI note of a degree of that scale, as the sequencer plays it, and `snap(midi)` the nearest note in it. `tune(semitones, oct)` turns a figure written as semitones above the tonic into MIDI notes in any key: it places the figure on the chapter's tonic nearest D4, where these sounds were tuned, and snaps each note into the mode. The fanfares for a solved step, a finished chapter and a prayer are written that way, so they sound in Chapter One, and on the title screen, exactly as before, and in every other chapter begin on its own tonic.
- **Where the sounds live.** The shared ensemble (`INST`, `DRUMS`), the ambience voices (`BEDS`, `CALLS`) and the sequencer stay in the engine, because every chapter and the title screen use them. The map's music lives in `shared/map.js`. A sound only one chapter needs lives in its level file:
  - an instrument or drum stroke in the score, `inst:{horn(f,t,d,v,down){...}}` or `drums:{x(t,v){...}}`, which wins over the engine's of the same name for that score only. It must write to `AU.out` (`AU.body` for a lyre-like body) and send to the room only with `tone({..., echo:1})`, so that its track's fade and room send cover it, as they do the engine's. Give an instrument `hand:true` (`Object.assign(fn,{hand:true})`) if it is played by hand, so that it lands a few milliseconds off the grid and takes the accents; drum strokes always are.
  - an ambience voice in what the level file returns, `calls:{name:[shortest gap, longest gap, (t, out)=>{...}]}` or `beds:{name:{type, f, q, lfo, depth, v}}`, made like the engine's and named unlike them. Its rooms name it in `amb` like any other; it is heard only while that chapter is played, on its map and end screen too, and fades when another chapter begins.
- **Sound starts** with the first press anywhere: browsers only allow it inside a click, tap or key press. Any later press wakes it again if the browser held it back or iOS interrupted it (after switching apps or locking the screen). The audio session is `ambient`, so iPhones and iPads keep the game quiet in Silent Mode; on those devices a note (`hush()`) says so, because Safari does not tell a page whether Silent Mode is on.
- **Artwork** is a painter per room: `paint` draws the backdrop once, `fore` an optional foreground layer, `draw` whatever changes with the story (items that can be taken, a sail going up), and `light` says how the room lights the people in it. The painters use the engine's toolkit: `plaster`, `ashlar`, `pave`, `wood`, `vessel`, `foliage`, `lightmap`, `shade` and so on. `pine` paints a pine as it grows out of rock, with a leaning trunk and its needles in clumps, and under twelve pixels high a pine far off, many of which make a wood. `gateView` paints the country seen through a city gate; a gate gives it the horizon (`hz`) and the sky of its own scene, so that the picture keeps one horizon, and each gate gets a road of its own.
- **Depth and shadows.** A room is drawn from the back to the front, so that the nearer hides the farther. The actors are sorted by the `y` of their feet, and so is everything that a room's `draw` places with `man`, `beast` or another `figure`: it waits in a queue (`DQ`) at the `y` of its feet until the room is put together. `depth(y, () => ...)` puts any other drawing in that queue; inside it figures are drawn at once, in the order written, so a team of oxen with its wagon and its yoke is one thing at one depth. Every `figure` with `sw > 0` also queues a shadow pass in `SQ` (`figShadow`), which runs immediately after `backdrop` and the room's `draw`—and before `DQ`—so all contact ovals and cast shadows lie on the floor and the wall underneath every actor and every foreground prop. `figShadow` reads `ART[id].light` (`LID`, set by `backdrop` for rooms and scenes and cleared for top-down lane maps): each lamp `[lx, ly, lr, col, lz, wy]` may give the floor depth `lz` under the light (defaulting to `wallY`) and its true wick height `wy` (defaulting to `ly`, leaving `lightAt` untouched), plus optional `wallY` (number or `x => y` for the wall-floor crease), `wall: [[x0, x1, yFloor, yTop], ...]` (solid back-wall spans where a shadow from a foreground lamp with `lz > y` bends upright without bleeding onto open doorways, windows or sky), and `sun: [dx, dy, strength]`. Up to the two strongest sources at `(x, y)` project softened two-pass `#181220` `multiply` silhouettes from a single reusable scratch canvas (`SC`, adding nothing to `SPR`), steady across `110 ms` flame flickers, scaled to zero in pitch dark, and skipped for figures perched high above `wallY` (such as Eutychus in the window). A thing that is painted into the backdrop and yet stands inside the walkable strip (`walk`), such as a stool, a low table or a heap of sailcloth, is named once more in the room's `props:[{y, shape(g, variant)}]`: `shape` fills its outline, without its shadow, and the engine cuts that outline out of the finished backdrop and lays the piece over whoever—and whatever shadow—lies behind it. What stands against the back wall, behind the strip, needs none of this. Where two stand level, the actor is in front. Cutscenes and a room's `front` are drawn in the order written.
- **Rooms and dialog** are tables of hotspots with their lines, and short async scripts for the puzzles and cutscenes. A hotspot's default verb (what a plain click does) is worked out by the engine: a door is opened, another exit is walked to, a person is talked to, something picked up with `take(...)` is picked up, and anything else is looked at. A hotspot can name its own with `def:'use'`, or with a getter when it changes with the story.
- **People** are hotspots with an `actor`. Besides `look` and `talk`, a person has a `give` script for the items that really matter to him (it returns `false` for the rest), a `show` table with his reply to every other item, keyed by item id, and a fallback `show['*']`, all in his own voice. Replies can be a line, a list of lines or a script. Using an item on a person is the same as giving it.
- **Wax tablets** are data too: `TABLETS` holds the chapter's `people` (name and note), `tasks` and `scripture` (a reference `ref` and a `gist`: the passage as Paul has noted it in his own words, never wording copied from a translation), each with an optional condition `if` on the story, and the chapter definition passes it on as `tablets`. A chapter may add `notes:{title, none, list:[{t, if}]}`, a third list with a heading of its own under the Scriptures, for what Paul writes down as he goes ("Seen in Athens", in Chapter Eleven). The overlay that shows them is `shared/tablets.js`, which the engine loads with `need('tablets')` the first time the tablets are opened. Anything else shared between chapters goes under `shared/` the same way.
- **A party** of playable characters is declared in the chapter definition: `party:['paul','barnabas']`. `P` is always Paul and the scripts use him by name; `P.called` is the name the story gives him, Saul until Acts 13:9 and Paul from there on (CONTRIBUTING section 2): a chapter sets it in its setup (Chapter Four sets Saul, and switches to Paul with the narration of Acts 13:9 as he faces Elymas), `setChapter` clears it, and the prayer, the swap button and the hand-over use `called` before `name` (`storyName`, which keeps the name of a pair played as one), so a new chapter before Acts 13:9 sets `P.called='Saul'` and a line that names Paul reads it too. `PC()` is whoever is being played, and `playAs(P)` hands control back before a cutscene. Each member has his own inventory: `home` says who usually carries an item (`{staff:'barnabas'}`), and `share` lists the items that can change hands. Another member does only what the level writes for him, in `as:{barnabas:{look, use, useWith, give, talk, show}}` on a hotspot or a person; otherwise he answers from his own `def` table, leaves the crafts to Paul (`noCraft`), and describes items in his own words (`ITEMS[id].by.barnabas`). A member who joins later stands in no room until then: the swap button names him and stays disabled, and the chapter's `away(id)` says from the story when he is still away, so that a code does not place him (Luke, in Chapter Eight). Paul himself may be away: in Chapter Ten `away('paul')` is true while he is on his way to the sea, Timothy is played (`playAs`), Paul stands in no room, and a code restores it so. A man who is to stand in the scenes before he can be played has two actors, one for each (`timothy10` and `tim10`).
- **Ids are shared.** Rooms, artwork, items, icons, looks and actors are kept in tables that every chapter writes into (`ROOMS`, `ART`, `ITEMS`, `ICON`, `LOOK`, `ACT`), so an id must be new in the whole game, not only in its file. A place or a person that comes back gets a second id: Chapter Seven's Lystra is `lystra7`, and its Timothy is `timothy7`, a man's height now. So do two places that only share a word: the house of Lois in Chapter Six is `house6`, because Chapter One has a `house` of its own. Otherwise the later chapter would redraw the earlier one's room, and a code for the earlier chapter would open the wrong scene. A chapter that brings the same person or thing back may repeat its look or its icon unchanged, as each does with Barnabas and his staff. The playtest watches every chapter register itself, and fails if one takes a room, a backdrop or an item from another.
- **A room that is heard, not seen** says `heard:true`, or a function of the story (the inner prison of Chapter Nine, until the earthquake). The names of its hotspots are then shown all the time, as the NAMES button shows them, and a click on a name acts on what it names: in the dark they are all there is to see. Such a room can be painted black; its hotspots are whatever can be heard, and its sounds are ambience voices that the story switches off one by one.
- **A close view** is a room in which nobody walks: the bolt of cloth and the scroll table of Chapter Ten, the row of altars of Chapter Eleven, the awning and the picture of the neck of land in Chapter Twelve, and the house and the counting board of Chapter Thirteen. Its strip to walk on is a single point below the picture (`walk:[160,160,236,236]`), so whoever is played stands out of sight and a click on a hotspot acts at once. Its hotspots are the parts of the thing, and what moves is drawn in `draw`, from a state kept on `G` (`G.bolt10`, `G.tab10`): that state is not in the safe-point code, only what has been solved is. The engine does not hand a click's place to a hotspot, so the bolt notes where the pointer is with a listener of its own on the view (`evPos`). A name that is painted on the canvas (what lies open under each scroll) is translated with `T` as it is drawn, and made to fit its width; the playtest checks that it does, in every language.
- **A room in which the player stands still.** A room's `walk` may be a getter. On the Areopagus of Chapter Eleven it is a single point in the picture while the speech lasts, so Paul stands in the midst and a click acts at once, and the whole rock afterwards. What is being set (what he begins from, and how he says it) is kept on `G` (`G.sp11`) and written at the foot of the view in `front`, made to fit its width as the names on the scroll table are.
- **A thought carried like a thing.** The four questions and the five grains of Chapter Eleven are items: an icon, a name and a look. A question is used on a hotspot (`useWith`) or given to a person, and the level decides what comes of it (`ask`, `RIGHT`, `WRONG`); the level's `COMBO` entries say what Paul thinks when two of them are used on each other. Nothing in the engine knows that they are thoughts.
- **Light that is worked out.** In the view of the awning in Chapter Twelve the sun is not painted. `dark(st, k, x, y)` says whether a point of the court is in shade for a setting `st` of the rope and the cloths, with the light falling `k` across for every one down: it follows the ray from that point back toward the sun, to the wall or to a stretch of rope that carries cloth. `lightField` asks it for every room pixel between the walls, once per setting, and the room's `draw` lays the result over the far wall and the floor; `judge` asks it for the benches and the place where Paul reads, and names what is wrong. In the rain `k` is 0 and only the slope of the rope counts. A hotspot there may give its place with a getter (`get x(){...}`), so that the cloth can be clicked where it now hangs.
- **Seasons.** A room's `variant()` may follow the story's season (`SEAS()` in `levels/chapter12.js`: autumn, winter, the spring of the games, summer, and the spring of the sailing), and the engine keeps one backdrop per variant. The level decides when a season turns (`turn`), and what has been promised for which season is data (`PROM`): the flag that is set when a promise is made, the flag when it is kept, and the note on the tablets.
- **A day in parts.** In Chapter Thirteen the story keeps the day and the part of it in its flags, and two small functions read them (`DAY()`, `PART()` in `levels/chapter13.js`: morning, midday, evening). A room's `variant()` follows them (`STREETV`, `SHOPV`), so the street and the workshop are painted once for each light. The sundial is a hotspot whose script moves the day on (`dial`, with a short scene of its own, `dial13`, in which the shadow turns: what stands still is painted once in three layers, the wall, the stone and the pointer, and what the sun moves is laid between them, so a frame is put together and dithered only when the shadow has moved); `OPEN13()` says what is still open in this part of the day, and while it names anything the shadow stays. Each morning, midday and evening sets one flag, so a safe-point code brings the player back to the same light.
- **What is missing.** In the synagogue of Chapter Thirteen the notes on the tablets are data (`HEARD`: the note, what Apollos says for it, and what the player may choose). All five stay on offer each time he has spoken, the ones already marked as well, and `think(who, q, opts)` offers choices of which the wrong ones are either spoken and answered (`by`) or only thought. Nothing in the engine knows that one note is never said.
- **A board that counts.** The counting board keeps its pebbles on `G` (`board()`: a digit for each of five places, and how far the reckoning has come), and only a finished reckoning is in the safe-point code. `lay` and `takeOff` add and remove one pebble and carry a fifth in a trough into the hollow and a second in a hollow into the next place; `reckon` compares the board with what has been said so far (`OWNERS`). The number letters beside the places are data too (`NUM13`), and the names of the hotspots are literal lists (`HOLLOW`, `TROUGH`) so that the strings check finds them.
- **A balance that is read.** The balance of Chapter Fifteen keeps where its four weights lie on `G` (`sc15()`: the round, and for each weight the board, the right pan or the left), and only a sealed bag is in the safe-point code. `tiltScale15` is the sign of the bag and what lies beside it less what lies across; `weigh` reads it. A weight's hotspot has no rectangle of its own: its `x`, `y`, `w` and `h` are getters on `weightBoxScale15`, which is also where the painter draws the weight, so a click always lands on the picture. One backdrop is kept for each round, and the beam, the bag and the weights are drawn over it every frame as they stand.
- **A night with no dead end.** The lamps of Chapter Fifteen are five numbers on `G` (`lamps15()`, with the jug beside them) and four flags for the sittings. `canLast(levels, jug, sittings)` tries every way of pouring what the jug holds and of sitting on, with a memo, and `pour` refuses a measure after which it finds none; so no state can be reached from which the night is lost, and the playtest walks every state to make sure. A code restores the lamps as the reference solution leaves them after that sitting (`AFTER`). The room's `light` is a function of the lamps, so the figures darken as the oil runs low, and the levels fall smoothly while Luke sits (a `tween` on the numbers themselves).
- **Told inside a room.** Midnight in Chapter Fifteen is one cutscene through three pictures. The upper room has a picture for each stage (`stage15()`: the meeting, midnight, after), and while the street is shown by night whoever is played stays where he was and is not seen, so that a code made at any moment of it restores the room at midnight, from where the stool leads on. A line spoken by someone who sits high in the picture has no room over his head: it is given to a voice that stands in no room (`EUV`), and so stands at the head of the view, while his mouth still moves.
- **A road of the chapter's own on the travel map.** `open15` lays a road out on the shared map without drawing it, and `go15` lets so many of its legs draw themselves, so that narration can stand between them. Two legs take waypoints of the chapter's own (`WAY15`), given as longitude and latitude and drawn the way `shared/map.js` draws its own; they belong in that file's `VIA` table and move there when it is next changed.
- **One room, one section.** In `levels/chapter15.js` each room's painting stands between two marker lines (`ART <room>: begin` and `end`), with every function in it named after the room, and its head says what it reads from the story and what is called from outside it. So a room can be painted anew, or seven at once, without touching the story or another room. The score stands between such markers too.
- **One played at a time.** Chapter Fourteen plays three people one after another, Eudoros, Damon and Theano, and Paul, and the story decides who: `place14` hands control to whoever the flags say, and the chapter sets `swap:false`, which hides the swap button (`swapBtn`, `swap`). The party is still named (`party`), because a wide code keeps the room of each member.
- **Heard, never written.** The crowd's cry of Acts 19:28, 34 is synthesized in `levels/chapter14.js` (`cry`, `shout`): many voices, each a buzz through three resonances that move with the vowel. Its vowels and rhythm are language data under the key `cry14`, as a sung line's notes are, and it is never shown on the screen.
- **A figure that sits where it stands.** An actor with `seat` set is drawn seated (`fr` −1) and does not walk; `setChapter` clears it. The figure painter gives a seated figure in a long robe its lap and its feet, and takes two more words in a look: `apron`, a working apron tied at the waist, and `sweat`, a cloth round the forehead (Paul at the bench in Chapter Thirteen; the study note on Acts 19:12).
- **Two played as one.** An actor may have a `mate`: `{fig, dx, talk}`, the id of a second look, how far beside the actor it stands, and whether it is speaking. `drawActor` paints the two together, a click on either is a click on the actor, and when such an actor prays, the prayer is worded for two (`PRAYERS2`). The mate's voice is an object of the level's own, with the mate's place (`PR` in Chapter Twelve), handed to `say` like any actor. Aquila and Priscilla are two such actors, as Timothy was in Chapter Ten: `aquila12` in the scenes and `aq12` when they can be played. A level that does not want things handed to a member of its party clears that member's `party` flag (`place12`), and then **Give** goes to the person's own `give` and `show`.
- **Hearing by nearness.** A room's `draw` runs every frame, so it can follow where the player stands: in the painted colonnade of Chapter Eleven it sets the mood (and so the tune) and starts a short scene when Paul comes to rest in another part of the room (`zone11`, `overhear`). It acts only while nothing else is going on (`!G.busy`).
- **Greek dress, statues and seated figures.** The figure painter takes a few more words in a look: `himation:1` wraps the mantle (`cloak`) round the body below the chest as well, down to the shins, the Greek outer garment for men and women; `fem:1` is a woman who goes bareheaded, her hair parted, bound with a band (`band`) and knotted at the back; `stone:1` is a statue, in the tones of its one material and with blank eyes, and `stub:1` breaks its far arm off at the elbow. A backdrop places a statue with the same painter (`statue()` in `levels/chapter11.js`). A man who sits is painted by the level (`sitter()` there), or directly by the figure painter with `fr < 0`, which sets the upper body lower over a seated lap and shins. In the prologue, `ges` 4 and 5 draw the near arm of a guide reaching back and the far arm of a blind man reaching forward, both forearms bent and slanting, so that two figures standing close hold hands (`levels/prologue.js`).
- **More words in a look.** `edge` is a band of another color along the mantle where it crosses the chest (the proconsul in Chapter Twelve). For the chapters ahead (`shared/cast.js`): `wet:1` and `torn:1|2|3` darken cloth/hair and notch ragged hems and rips for shipwreck survivors (`barefoot:1` removes sandals); Roman military dress adds `armorStyle` (`'seg'` for overlapping iron strips, `'mail'` for chain mail, `'cuirass'` for a molded officer breastplate with `pteruges` and `sash`), `helmStyle` (`'galea'`, `'crest'` transverse centurion crest, `'plume'` longitudinal Praetorian crest, colored by `crest`), `milBelt:1`, `caligae:1`, `offCloak:1` (officer cloak over the left shoulder), `toga:1` (formal Roman toga), `gladius:1|'left'` (sheathed, without crossguard) and `pilum:1`.
- **Speaking figures.** `say` reads the line it shows into a `cadence`: a row of mouth shapes with their times, and the clauses they fall into. While the line stands, `moves` turns that into what each figure does at this moment, as `[mouth, far hand, eyes shut, a sum of MV]`, and `drawActor` hands it to `person(g,x,y,look,dir,fr,talk,bow,ges,blink,mv)`: `talk` is the mouth (0 shut, 1 parted, 2 open, 3 wide, 4 round, the last two with the chin down), and `mv` a sum of `MV.dip`, `lift`, `fwd`, `back`, `brow`, `glance` and `lean`. Every pose is still one cached sprite, so the store holds up to 500 and lets the oldest go. A level writes nothing for any of this: the words on the screen are the data, in every language. A line that is sung hands its notes to `say` as `sung:[[from,to,vowel],...]` in milliseconds, and `choir:[actors]` for those who sing with the speaker or without one (Chapter Nine's `sung`). A script that sets `talk` on an actor by hand, while the narrator tells of a cry, gets a babbling mouth. `G.with` is the person the played character talks with or shows something to, for as long as that script runs; `cur.to` is the one a line is spoken to: `say(a, line, {to:b})` names him, and `{to:null}` names nobody (a crowd, a reading, a cry), so that the speaker faces the way the scene has him. Without `to`, a line of the played character goes to `G.with` or to the last one he spoke with in this scene, and anyone else's to the last one he spoke with, or else to the played character. Each line turns the two to each other and records it in `G.turn` (person to `{to, dir}`), which `cut` and `enterRoom` clear; so a chapter names the one spoken to where two others turn to each other, and the rest follows. The engine never makes a listener nod: a nod is a yes (CONTRIBUTING section 4). The far hand's phrases (`ges`: 1 raised, 2 half raised and open, 3 on the chest) and the lean are chosen in `moves` from each clause's end mark and a hash of the line. `facing(o)` is the way an actor is drawn: toward the one he talks with in `G.turn`, or, if he is `G.with`, toward the played character, in the picture only, so `dir` stays what the scripts made it; a script that sets his `dir` or walks him ends the turn, and `noTurn:1` on an actor keeps him as the scene has him. A mate turns on the spot by herself (`mateFacing`); her `voice` is the object that speaks her lines. Walking up to a person, the played character stands 22 pixels to one side, or the other, or a step farther out, wherever no figure (a mate included) stands within 14 pixels across and 11 in depth. The playtest checks every line spoken in a room (`talked` in the harness): speaker and listener face each other, and neither stands within 10 pixels of another figure. A movement that says something is directed by a script and never given by the engine: `nod(a)`, `shakeHead(a)` and `handOnChest(a, ms)` (each a promise that resolves when it is over), `say(a, line, {act:'nod'})`, or the chapter's table of lines that are spoken with one, `const ACTING={nod:[...],shake:[...],heart:[...]}`, returned as `acting` in its definition; each entry is a line in English exactly as it stands in the script, and the text check fails on an entry that is no line of the chapter. `OPTS.mov` 0 is calm movement, which still gives a directed gesture. `node tools/playtest.mjs cast` checks that each of some 4,700 poses is one piece and saves `cast-talk.png`, the frames enlarged; the stop `speak` holds a line at the moment its question ends.
- **Walking.** Everybody walks at `WALK_V` (48 room pixels a second) unless a script gives an actor a `speed` of his own, easing in and out over the first and last 14 room pixels of a room walk. `gaitOf(look)` is his step: its length `S` in drawing units, the least time `T` two steps take, how far the arms swing, and the stance share (`stance`, 0.56 for a 12 % double-support overlap), worked out from the look (a long robe, a boy, grey hair, a slim beardless youth, a spear). `strideOf(look, v)` is how far two steps carry him: `S / (2 * stance)` room pixels where the feet stay planted, and slightly more where that would mean more than two steps in `T` seconds (under 4 % slip at 48). `update` keeps the phase of each walker's stride in `a.gait` and advances it by the ground covered; `gaitPose(look, phase, amp)` turns a phase into anatomical hip-knee-ankle legs, feet, pendulum arms, four-beat weight bob with head lag, subtle forward lean and shoulder counter-sway, dynamic shadow width (`walkSw`), and flaring cloth, and `person` paints it from a centered hip pivot (`-4`). The frame is `fr`: 0 stands, 4 to 19 are the sixteen moments of a full step, 20 to 35 of a shorter one and 36 to 51 of a half step, on which a walk begins and ends; 1, 2 and 3 remain as three of those moments, for a figure that a scene has posed in mid-step. A scene that moves figures itself asks `walkFr(look, distance, speed)` for the frame (the road of Chapter Three, the parting in Chapter Seven, the escort in Chapter Nine). The donkey has the same kind of walk: `donkey` takes `fr` 4 to 19 as the sixteen frames of a four-beat cycle, and a scene asks `beastFr(distance, speed)` for the frame; `node tools/playtest.mjs beasts` checks every frame and saves `beasts-walk.png`. The oxen and the mule stand in every scene and have no walk of this kind. `SFX.step` sounds as a foot lands. `node tools/playtest.mjs cast` checks some 7,600 walking frames for one that has come apart and saves `cast-walk.png`; the stop `stride` holds Paul as a heel lands.
- **A look that changes with the story, and with the years.** An actor whose dress changes (Paul and Silas without their cloaks) is given another look and a short `wear` that names it, so that the poses of each look are kept apart; every chapter's start gives everyone his own look back, clears the sprite cache (`SPR`), and sets `LOOK.paul` (`paulLook(ch)`) and `LOOK.barnabas` for that chapter's point in Acts. For aging across the years, a look may also say `receding:1` or `thin:1` (the stages of hair loss between `hair` and `bald`), `crop:1` (hair clipped short, or `cropThin` when combined with `thin:1`), `shortBeard:1` (a young man's first close beard), `fullBeard:1` (between `beard` and `longBeard`), and `grey` (a color for the highlight of the hair and the beard, before the whole head turns grey).
- **A sung line** is scheduled outside the score, on a bus of its own, while its text stands on the screen (`sung()` in `levels/chapter9.js`); a line that the player skips breaks off. The line is written as its notes, syllable by syllable: the degree of the scale, a dot, the length in steps and the vowel, as in `0.2a 2.1@ 4.2o`. That string is translated like any other line, because the vowels and the rhythm follow the words: each language gives its own under the same key.
- **A passage on the tablets** is a `gist`: a note in Paul's own words with its reference, shown without quotation marks, because a paraphrase is never presented as a quotation (CONTRIBUTING section 1). Where a character says a passage, he says that same note with its reference (`quote(k)` in the level files).
- **Choices.** `choose(opts, o)` shows the lines and returns the place in `opts` of the one picked. Where a line stands must not give it away (CONTRIBUTING section 2), so the lines are shown in a mixed order: each is ranked by a hash of its key (`mixRank`, with the seed `MIX`), which is the same every time and in every language, and keeps the others in their order when one drops out. `o.order` shows a menu of questions as it is written (`talkTree` does); `o.keep` keeps so many lines at the end, for a line that only leaves or only asks to hear more; `o.by` gives the keys to rank by when the lines are put together from parts (the passages of Chapter Two). The seed was chosen so that, over all the chapters, the right line falls about as often first, in the middle and last, no chapter leans to one place, and no sequence (the psalm of Chapter Nine, Apollos's points, the reasons at Troas, the message from Beroea) comes out in its own order or backwards. The playtest picks a line by its place as the level gives it (`data-i` on each button), and fails on a choice that is mixed by translated text.
- **Topics** are what Paul learns in a chapter: `TOPICS` lists them, each with its question and a condition on the flags, for example `{id:'sail',q:'captains_sail_is_torn',if:()=>F.metCapt}`. A person's `ask` table holds his answer to each topic he can be asked about. `talkTree` adds the topics that are open to its own questions, each asked once.

**Text and translations.** The code holds no text. Where a line is shown it holds a key: a look line on its hotspot, a reply in a person's `show` table, a line in a script, `say(P,'i_untie_the_pouch')`. The text of every key stands in the language files under `lang/`, English as well, and the engine looks it up as it is shown, with `T(key)`: `say` and `narr`, `choose`, the status line, the top bar, the Tab labels, the tablets, the map and the overlays all do this, so a level never calls `T` itself, except for a line put together from parts, which is a template with its own key: `T('it_still_needs_list',{list})`, whose English is "It still needs {list}.". The game's logic only ever sees keys, so flags, hotspot names and safe-point codes are the same in every language.

A key says what its line is, in lowercase words joined by `_` (`cricket_somewhere_in_the_wall`, `on_our_way_to_the_place_acts_16_16`); a single word may be its own key (`Silas`, `awl`). It is written once, with the line, and never renamed, even when the English changes later. The same English line has the same key wherever it stands, and no two lines share one. Each language is a folder under `lang/`: `main.js` for the engine and `shared/`, `prologue.js`, and `chapter1.js` and so on, each loaded with its chapter. English (`lang/en/`) is always loaded beside the player's language and is what a missing line falls back to. An English file is a table from each key to its line; in every other language an entry is the translation and the fingerprint of the English line it was made from:

```js
TEXTS('en',1,{
  "spool_of_thread":"spool of thread",
  "waxed_thread_said":"Waxed thread. Good and strong.",
  ...
});
TEXTS('de',1,{
  "spool_of_thread":["Garnspule","1p52ovf"],
  "waxed_thread_said":["Gewachster Faden. Gut und stark.","1p06myz"],
  ...
});
```

A new line: write its key in the code, its English in `lang/en`, its text in every other language, and record the fingerprints with `node tools/strings.mjs --confirm de 1 <key>`. A changed English line keeps its key, and its translations become stale: `node tools/strings.mjs --show de 1 --stale` lists them beside the new English; rewrite each that no longer says the same, then confirm it. `node tools/strings.mjs --check` (which the playtest runs first) reports text written in the code instead of a key, an English line no part of the game uses, a line missing from a language, a translation whose English line is gone or has changed since it was made, a `{slot}` added in a translation, English book names in a reference, and pronunciation marks copied from the Bible text. Then the playtest plays the whole game in each language and fails on any key shown without its text, or shown as it is, and on any text that overflows its box. The top bar counts as such a box: a chapter's name that is too long to stand beside its code wraps into a second line, and the playtest reports it (Chapter Twelve has the longest codes, so its German name in the bar is short). To add a language: add its line to `LANGS` in `index.html` (`fr:{native:'Français',dir:'lang/fr/'}`), copy `lang/de` to `lang/fr` and replace the translations, telling every verse in that language in your own words and checking it against that language's Study Edition (CONTRIBUTING sections 1 and 8), and confirm each part (`--confirm fr <part> --stale`) once its lines are written. Nothing else in the engine changes.

**The title's height.** The title screen alone uses the whole height of the window under the top bar: `VT`, 210 room pixels, which is the view, the status line and the panel together. Its overlay carries the class `title`, and `#wrap.title` gives the view that height and takes the status line and the panel out of the layout. The canvas is that tall always; a room is painted into its upper 144 pixels, which is all the view shows in play. The title's picture is a scene of Chapter One (`scenes.title`, named by `TITLE`), baked at that height with `bake(w, fn, q, h)`: the ground below the wall is painted first and only below row 144, and then the wall's own painter runs, so that the upper part is the wall's backdrop pixel for pixel and nothing moves when the title is left (the playtest compares the two). A menu or the code box opened over the title covers all of it. No other screen is taller than the view.

**The phone layout.** `fit()` chooses the layout: the phone layout on a screen that is touched and has no mouse (`(hover: none) and (pointer: coarse)`) and whose shorter side is at most 500 CSS pixels, or as the player chose it in the options (`OPTS.lay`: `auto`, `phone`, `classic`, kept in `paul.opts`). On a phone `<html>` carries the class `phone`, and `dock()` moves the verbs into `#lcol` and the inventory into `#rcol`, the **≡** and **NAMES** buttons to the top of the left column and **SKIP** onto the field; off the phone each goes back where it came from, so the classic layout is untouched. The view is then the whole screen, and the picture lies in it as `LENS` says: `k` CSS pixels to the room pixel, its corner at `tx`, `ty`, and `F`, the field between the columns. `lensTick()` works it out for every frame. The canvas is sized and moved to it, and everything that turns a place on the screen into a place in the picture asks `LENS` too: `evPos`, `framePos` (the travel map), `toClient` (the playtest's clicks), the speech lines (`posSpeechPh`), the labels (`drawTags`) and the item that flies to the inventory. The picture fills the field from top to bottom (`fill`), except a close view, where whoever is played stands below the picture, and the travel map, which are fitted whole (`fit`); the title is fitted into the screen with a strip of timber at each side (`PH.kT`, `--tb`), where **ENTER CODE** and **≡** stand one over the other in the upper right corner (`#ttop`), as the title's last line says. In `fill` it follows whoever is played, or whoever speaks while a line stands, plus `PH.pan`, which a finger sets and which goes back to nothing when he walks or someone speaks. In a room wider than the picture the room's own view (`G.cam`) follows the same point (`update`). Two fingers set `PH.pz` (1 to 3; a close view `PH.pzFit`), and `lensAim` keeps the point between them where it is; a lane map keeps its own zoom (`G.zoom`). The menu, the safe-point box, an end screen, the tablets and the narrator's scroll make the field the whole screen (`#wrap.wide`), and the columns slide out. The sizes the stylesheet needs are CSS variables on `<html>`: `--cw`, `--ci` (the safe margin of a phone with its camera in the screen, read from `env(safe-area-inset-*)` through `#safe`), `--cs`, `--ts`, `--fl`, `--fr`, `--fw`, and `--fx`, `--fy`, `--fk` for the whole picture fitted into the field (the scroll). The page itself is never zoomed on a phone: the viewport is held, and Safari's own pinch is stopped. Installed on the home screen of an iPhone the game is laid out up to the top edge of the screen (`apple-mobile-web-app-status-bar-style: black-translucent`); the safe margin at the top (`env(safe-area-inset-top)`) keeps the columns' buttons, the status line, SKIP, the notices, the overlays and the narrator's lines clear of a status bar. There Safari reports a page that is short by the status bar's height, and would leave a black band at its foot, so `fit()` makes the game as tall as the screen itself (`--vh`, from `screen` while `navigator.standalone`). The playtest's phone stops (`phoneall` and its pictures) are seen on a phone held sideways, 852 by 393, with the safe margins of an iPhone; `phoneportrait` held upright.

**The opening picture.** `title()` shows `shared/splash.js` the first time it runs after the page is loaded, and never again until the page is loaded anew (`G.splash`: undefined before, 1 while it waits, 2 while the overture plays, 0 after). The title's overlay is laid out as always and carries the class `splash`, which hides everything but the name and the line under it, so that the name stands where it will stand over the chapters. The first click calls `audioInit()` and sets the overture as the score; the score names the title's music as `then`, and the sequencer (`stepOn`) plays a score with `then` once and goes on to that one. A timer, not the audio clock, ends the splash, so that it ends with the sound off too. `leave()` in the splash cross-fades its picture into the title's scene. Chapter One's score has an overture of its own (its mood `ancient`, which the title plays when the player comes back to it from a game, and the prologue at its end); after the opening picture the title's music begins in `calm` instead, so that no second overture follows the first. If the prologue is started while the opening picture's overture still plays, `startPrologue()` sets Chapter One's score and the overture ends there. If the file cannot be loaded the title opens as it did before. The overture's horn (`INST.horn`) is one of the engine's voices, so that a chapter may use it too. The playtest passes through the splash at the start of every run; the stop `splash` shows it.

**The title logo.** `shared/logo.js` paints the game's name on the title screen: raised gold capitals that stand taller toward the middle, so that the name stands under an arch, with the subtitle between two rules beneath. There is no font file, and the letters are the game's own. Each one is a skeleton of strokes (`GL`), written with a broad pen held at an angle, which gives the thick and thin of Roman capitals, and finished with serifs. That makes a mask of whole pixels, two to the room pixel like the backdrops, and the gold, the raised side, the outline and the shadow are painted from the mask, lit from the upper right, where the moon stands. `GL` holds the whole alphabet with Ä, Ö, Ü and the question mark, because the chapters' title cards are painted with it too (the name of the chapter, and "Chapter One" beneath it). A line with an umlaut gets room above it for the dots. A language whose title or chapter names need another letter adds its glyph there; until it does, and whenever the file is missing, the title and the cards stand as plain text. The canvas keeps the words for a screen reader.

**The narrator's scroll.** `narr` (that is, `say` with no speaker) looks whether the picture is dark: the view is faded out (`fade(1)`), or the scene is the engine's `DARK`, which a chapter sets as `G.scene` for what is told and not shown. If it is, `shared/scroll.js` is loaded, the scroll comes up on a canvas of its own above the darkened view with a soft rustle (`SFX.unroll`, once for a passage and not for every line), and the line is set on it in ink (`fit`: the largest of four sizes of writing that fits, with every reference kept whole). The room that a script enters behind the dark is not disturbed. `fade`, a spoken line and `endScreen` wait until the scroll has gone down, and a scroll left with no line on it goes down after a moment. So a chapter writes nothing for it: it fades out and tells, as before, and a passage takes about a second longer than it did. A line that should stand plain in the dark all the same is told with `{plain:1}`. When the screen is faded to white instead (`fade(1, ms, 1)` on the road to Damascus in `levels/prologue.js`), `inDark()` is false, the scroll stays down, and `say` gives the line `.line.white`, setting the words in dark ink straight on the warm white screen. The picture is put together once from layers (`build`): the room and the roll are finished like a backdrop, and the hands and sleeves are painted in greys and set in a scale of tones (`tones`). A line that is too long for the roll even in the smallest writing is noted in `SHARED.scroll.over`, and a playthrough fails on it in any language. The stops `scroll` and `scrolllong` check how the scroll comes and goes and show it with a usual line and with the longest one: look at both in German too (`de:scroll`).

**The planned chapters.** `PLANNED` in `index.html` lists the chapters that are planned and not built (issue #47), each with its label, its number as a word (`short`, for the line that opens the list) and the part of Acts it will tell. The title screen shows them under one line at the foot of the chapter list, **Chapters Sixteen to Twenty-Five · in preparation**, dimmed and closed; a click on that line toggles the class `open` on `.chs` and shows them. A planned chapter is named by its reference, not by a working title, because the episode is agreed only in its issue before it is built (CONTRIBUTING section 2), and it cannot be clicked. Nothing else reads `PLANNED`: those chapters have no level file, no salt and no codes. When one is built, its entry leaves `PLANNED` and it enters `CHAP` with its title; the line then counts from the next. The stop `plans` shows the list open.

**The prologue.** `levels/prologue.js` (with `lang/en/prologue.js` and `lang/de/prologue.js`) is loaded on demand with `loadPrologue()` when the player chooses **Prologue · The Road to Damascus** at the head of the title screen's list (`PRO`). It shares Chapter One's score (`levels/chapter1.js`), which includes the ten-bar overture in `ancient` (`overture:{mood:'ancient',bars:10,to:'calm'}`: the sequencer switches to `'calm'` at Bar 11 on the title screen, and in the prologue's last picture `AU.step = 96` starts at the four-bar climax and cross-fades into `'calm'` as Chapter One begins), the `'stones'` mood outside Jerusalem, `'harp'` in the house of Judas, and `silenceNow()` when the light from heaven cuts off every sound at once. While the prologue runs, `#skip` is shown in the top bar; clicking it sets `G.proSkip` and starts Chapter One directly (`CHAP[1].start(false)`), whereas letting the prologue run through flows into `CHAP[1].start(true)`, which omits the two opening background lines already told in the prologue. Its playtest stops are `procard`, `pro1`, `pro2`, `pro3`, `pro4`, `pro5`, `pro7`, `pro8`, `pro8b` and `pro9`.

**The installed app.** `sw.js` and `shared/pwa.js` make the game installable and playable offline. The page loads `shared/pwa.js` only when it is served over http(s), never from disk, so the playtest's pages and a copy opened from a folder are unchanged. It paints the icon from the P of the title logo and builds the manifest as a blob: there is no icon file and no manifest file. The service worker takes the network first, so that a player always gets one version's files together, and falls back to what it has kept; the page asks it to keep every chapter, shared file and language file ahead of time. A new file in `shared/` is added to `FILES` in `shared/pwa.js` (the playtest fails if it is missing); chapters and languages are found from `CHAP` and `LANGS`. An installed icon is `docs/screenshots/pwa-icon.png`.

**The lane map.** `shared/lanes.js` makes a room of its own kind: a city seen at a slant from above, taller and wider than the view, which follows whoever is played across and up and down (`G.camY`; `ART[id].h` is the room's height, and `camAt` places the view), and which the player may zoom (`G.zoom`, glided to `G.zoomTo`; `zoomOf` and `zoomNow` say what a room is shown at, and the click, the labels, the speech and the flying item all follow it). A chapter that has one names the file in its line of the manifest (`needs:['lanes']`), and `loadLevel` loads it before the chapter's own file; the chapter then calls `SHARED.lanes.make(id, def)` with the city's size, its lanes as lines with a width (`lanes`), its squares and courts (`open`), the ground it paints itself (`keep`: a theater, a hill, a wall, a harbor), its covered passages (`bridges`: a house over a lane, with an archway), what it paints on a house front (`marks`: a door), what stands in the way (`solid`, and `block` for one variant only), the jams of people in a variant (`jams`: narrow streets packed with people who cannot get through themselves, drawn standing and shuffling, and blocked for everyone), what blocks a way in a variant with the line that names it (`obstacles`: whoever is played says it when a click stops him before one, or he steers into it; a jam's `say` does the same), the size of the houses and their doors and windows (`lot`, `door`, `win`, `winH`, `doorH`, `flat`, `ew`), the crowd of each variant (`crowd`: where people make for, and where they come from, in waves, by a gate or a street or out of the doors of the houses), the places it wants to know when he walks into them (`zones`) and the places whose distance along the lanes it asks for (`targets`). The houses are laid out by the file itself, in rows of boxes cut by the lanes, each with its height (`def.height` may set it), its front, door and windows. The ground is the room's backdrop (`paintGround`, then the chapter's `paint`); each house is a sprite of its own, its roof as high above its ground as its front is tall, cut into strips of columns that take their place among the people by the row of their front, as a room's props do, and a house fades (`FADE`) while whoever is played is behind it. The engine hands the map what differs from a room through `ROOMS[id].plan`: `route` (a click walks along the lanes by the shortest way he knows: the lanes he has seen on the screen where no house hides them, and those within eighteen cells of where he has been; where those do not reach, he goes as near as they take him without a long way round, the way is `partial`, and the engine does not act on what was clicked; a script walks by every lane), `tick` (the crowd, what he comes to know, the fading, and the arrow keys or W A S D, which steer on the map only), `draw` (the dots of the way and its diamond, the houses), `drawActor` (the figures), `pace` and `stride` (a runner runs, anyone else walks; the frame of a walk or a run comes from the distance covered), `fig` (the size of a figure, for the labels, the speech and a click on a person) and `face` (he turns to what he walked up to, up or down the map too). The crowd presses whoever is played back when three of it come up against him within a second and a half, walking against a packed street, across it, or standing in it; in a gap between waves, or in a back lane, nobody does. The chapter may have him say so (`press`). `M.dist(name, x, y)` is the way along the lanes to a place, which the room's sound can follow: in the demo the roar of the crowd dies down as the runner nears the house. The searches keep their costs in 64 bits: a cost kept in 32 and rounded up is found better again and again, and the search never ends. Chapter Fourteen's Ephesus is the map the playtest checks the engine on: its plan follows *Insight* ("Ephesus") and the Study Edition's media on Acts 19:29 for the theater, the harbor street and the agora, and the maintainer's notes for the grid (`grid:true` lets its streets cross; the playtest then counts the crossings instead of failing on them). The chapter hands the playtest one pass of the map at once (`CHAP[14].lanes(1)`, the runner in the surge; `lanes(2)`, Paul after the uproar), which it plays (`lanesall`, in every language) and pictures (`lanes`, `lanes2`, `lanesfolk`, and `lanesmap`, the whole map in both passes with the ways the test walks). A zone (`zones`) fires whenever whoever is played stands in it, placed or walking: the court where Paul is staying ends the runner's pass, so a test that places the runner keeps out of it. A member of a party who is not Paul is given a hotspot's handlers through `h.as[his id]`, on a lane map as in any room.

**The travel map.** `shared/map.js` holds the geography, and its own music: the sting and the chime as a leg arrives are data (`STING`, `CHIME`), semitones above the tonic and an octave, played through the engine's `tune()`, so they follow each chapter's key without naming one. It also holds `PLACES` (each city's longitude and latitude, and the side its name goes on), the coasts, islands, lakes and ranges, and `VIA`, waypoints for legs that should not run straight. A chapter declares the way out of it as `route:[['jerusalem','caesarea','new'],['caesarea','tarsus','new sea']]`, with the earlier legs first and `new` on the ones to draw now, and calls `await journey()` in its ending. Check a new leg against the others: the playtest fails if two routes cross. A name sits on the side its place gives it (`lab`, and `dy` to raise or lower it). Where that side would lie across one of a chapter's routes, the chapter says where the name goes on its own map: `labels:{salamis:{lab:'r',dy:4.2}}`. A road travelled a second time is not drawn a second time: two dotted lines along one curve cross each other. Chapter Six ends by showing its roads at once (`journey(route,{instant:1})`), moving the light from city to city (`state.pulse`), and drawing only the new legs with `go(from,to)`. Chapter Ten tells its road on the map three times and calls `SHARED.map.journey` itself, each time with the legs and the places of the names for that map (`{labels:{thessalonica:{lab:'t',dx:-7}}}`: a name may be moved sideways with `dx` as well as up or down with `dy`, and set small with `sz`); `night:1` darkens the window for a road travelled by night. Chapter Twelve opens on a map with two cities and no road, because the account names none: `journey([], {seen:['athens','corinth'], pulse:'corinth'})` shows places that no leg reaches and says where the light stands. It ends by looking back (`lookBack`): five maps with `instant:1`, journey by journey, so that no two roads of the twelve chapters cross on one of them. The playtest checks each of this chapter's maps for names that cross a line, run out of the window or lie on another name (`SHARED.map.window()` says where the window lies); a place that was only passed through may keep its place on a road without a dot or a name (`labels:{amphipolis:{hide:1}}`). A line told over such a map is said with `narr(text,{low:1})`, which stands it at the foot of the view, because the road runs along the window's upper edge.

The map is larger than the screen. Everything on it keeps its coordinates, and a chapter whose road leaves the usual window names another one: `view:'north'` (Chapters Five, Six and Seven) or `view:'aegean'` (Chapter Eight, and the chapters in Macedonia and Achaia that follow it). Chapter Ten looks through `view:'west'`, the Aegean window moved a little further west, so that Thessalonica and Beroea have room for their names. A view is declared in `VIEWS` as the distance its window lies to the right of and below the usual one, in whole pixels; `SEA` says where the name of the sea stands in each. The coast, the islands and the ranges are painted once per view. A new view may need coast that the others never showed: the Aegean shore north of Miletus was added for the northern one, and for the Aegean one the Black Sea shore, the Propontis, Thrace, Macedonia, Greece and the islands between. A place with `hide:1` is a point on a road and nothing more: a leg may start or end there, and neither a dot nor a name is drawn. Chapter Eight uses two, where the account names a region and no town.

**A hub chapter** (Chapters Four, Five, Six and Seven) is a chapter whose rooms are places on the map that the player travels between by clicking. It declares them in its definition, by their ids in `shared/map.js`, each with the room it leads to and where Paul stands on arrival:

```js
rooms:['seleucia','salamis','synagogue','paphos'],
places:{

  salamis:{room:'salamis',x:150,y:122,dir:1,if:()=>G.room==='seleucia'},
  paphos:{room:'paphos',x:150,y:124,dir:1,if:()=>G.room==='salamis'&&F.spoke,no:'Paphos lies at the far end of the island.'}},
```

and gives the exits that lead out of town `exit:travel`, or an exit of its own that checks what the story still needs and then calls `travel()`. `travel()` opens the map with those places as dots; hovering lights a name, a click draws the way there and enters that room, and Esc or a right-click stays. A place can say when the way to it is open (`if`) and what the character says when it is not (`no`: a line, a list of lines or a script), and `hide` keeps it off the map. The chapter's `way()` lists the legs already travelled, which the map shows, and its `arrive(to, from)` runs after the journey and before the room appears: it brings the rest of the party along, and may tell of the journey, as the crossing to Salamis is told. It may also enter another room than the place's own: in Chapter Five the road to Antioch comes down to a ford first. A place can stand for more than one room: in Chapter Six, Lystra is the open place by the well, the ground outside the gate, or the house of Lois, so its `room` is a getter that follows the story. A place that is not in `PLACES` yet is added there with its coordinates. A hub chapter will have more than three rooms, so it gets wide codes automatically; if it may grow, it reserves room with `codeBits` from the start.

**The pointer.** The mouse pointer over the view is painted in the engine: `PTR` is its pixel map, on the grid of the view's canvas pixels, and `pointerArt(u)` paints it with a dark contour round all eight neighbours of every painted pixel. `fit()` hands the layout's scale to `pointer(s)`, which sets it as the view's CSS cursor, at the display's own density where the browser takes an image set, and paints it anew only when its size changes. The browser's `crosshair` is not used: it is thin and easily lost in a dark scene, and it is a cross. `pointerKind` chooses what it shows: while a verb is chosen, that verb's picture from `PTRS` (`look`, `pickup`, `use`, `open`, `talk`, `give`), with the point that clicks given in `PTRP`, and painted brighter (a kind ending in `-hot`) while it rests on a hotspot; with no verb, the arrow, brighter over a thing or a person, and an arrow that points the way over an exit. The playtest checks that every verb's picture is one piece inside its contour and stays within the 128 pixels a browser takes for a cursor; the stop `cursors` shows them all enlarged, to look for crosses.

**Autosave.** The save is the safe-point code, not a second format: `codeBtn()` writes the string it shows to `localStorage` under `paul.save`, whenever the player is in a room outside a cutscene (so it always names a point they could have written down). The title screen reads it back with `saved()` and, if it still decodes, leads with **Continue**, which `resume()` restores the way typing the code does. `endScreen()` replaces the save with the next chapter's first code (or clears it, after the last chapter). A browser that refuses storage is fine: the game plays on and the code screen does not claim a save. Because the save is a code, every promise below holds for it too, and nothing else may go into it without a format the decoder can tell apart.

**Saved games.** Everything else the player keeps lives under its own key, `paul.saves`, as JSON: `{far:{code,t}, last:{code,t}, slots:[{code,t} or null ×4]}`, with `t` the time it was kept. Each entry is a safe-point code, read back through `decode()` (`saves()`), so a damaged entry shows as empty and the promises below cover every slot. `autosave()` passes each point reached to `reached()`, which updates `last` and moves `far` only when `farther()` says the new point is farther: a later chapter, or in the same chapter more of the story's flags set (`nflags()`). A point restored by a typed code (`G.typing` → `G.typed`) does not move it; the next point reached from there does. Only the player deletes `far`. The panel is `drawSaves()` inside the options menu, its buttons go through `slotDo()`, which asks in the row before overwriting, deleting or loading over a game in play, and `restore()` loads a code the way **ENTER CODE** does.

**Hidden treasures.** Each chapter hides one (CONTRIBUTING section 2). Its entry in `CHAP` names it and gives `at`, a safe-point code a little before it: `treasure:{name:'The marks by the door',at:'7-...'}`. The chapter calls `treasure()` where it is found; the engine keeps it under `paul.treasures`, plays `SFX.treasure` and shows the notice (`notice()`, the element `#found`), once. Nothing else changes: no flag, no item, no code. That key holds a treasure code and nothing else (`treasCode()`, `treasDecode()`): `HT` and nine characters, 36 bits, one per chapter, masked and checked with nine bits as a wide code is. A safe-point code of that length begins with two digits, so `decode()` never reads a treasure code and `treasDecode()` never reads a safe-point code. `keepTreasures()` only ever adds. The screen is `drawTreasures()` inside the options menu (`openMenu('treasures')` from the title); a place not found shows nothing, and in a chapter that `reachedCh()` says was reached it leads back through `restore(at, 1)`, which restores the point as a typed code is restored, so the farthest point does not move. **ENTER CODE** takes a treasure code too and adds what it holds. The playtest's stop `treasures` checks that every chapter has a treasure, a point and steps in `TSTEPS` (in `tools/playtest-harness.html`), finds each from its point, checks that the story's state is unchanged and that it is announced once, and shows the screen; `treN` stops as Chapter N's treasure is found.

**Safe-point codes.** A code begins with its chapter's number: nothing for Chapter One, one digit for Chapters Two to Nine (`9-XXX-XXX-XXX`), and two digits from Chapter Ten on (`10-XXX-XXX-XXX`). Without its hyphens a code is therefore a multiple of three characters long, or one more, or two more, and the decoder tells the three apart by that alone, so every code that ever worked still does. A chapter whose state fits in 19 flags and three rooms, with Paul alone, gets the original six-character codes. Any other chapter (a party, more flags, more rooms) gets wide codes of 9, 12 or more characters, always a multiple of three, so the decoder tells the two apart by length. A wide code packs, from the lowest bit: the flags, each member's room, the member being played, and for each item in `share` the member who carries it; then it is masked and checked with 9 bits salted by the chapter, like the short codes. Every field has a fixed width, so a chapter can grow without breaking its codes: room for 19 flags and two bits per room (four rooms), unless the chapter needs more or reserves more with `codeBits:{flags, rooms, share}`. Never reorder a chapter's `flags`, `rooms` or `share`; only append, within those widths.

**The trailer.** `tools/trailer/` makes the game's trailer from the game itself, in English and German, about 89 seconds at 1920 x 1080 (needs Node 22 or newer, Google Chrome and ffmpeg):

```bash
node tools/trailer/trailer.mjs music        # the score, to tools/trailer/out/music.wav (about a minute)
node tools/trailer/trailer.mjs video        # the trailer, to tools/trailer/out/trailer-en.mp4 and trailer-de.mp4 (about four minutes each)
node tools/trailer/trailer.mjs stills de    # three frames of every shot on one sheet, to check a shot quickly (name shots to take only those)
```

The score, "The Road in the Light" (`tools/trailer/score.js`), is the Chapter One theme of the overture in D Hijaz, scored for a trailer and chosen by the maintainer from drafts. `tools/trailer/music.js` renders it offline on the engine's own instruments, with a few voices of its own (a string ensemble, wordless voices, great drums, timpani, low brass, risers and impacts) in a long hall. `music` prints the level of every bar, and `levels` prints each track on its own, so that the balance can be checked without listening. The film is shot in the game: `tools/trailer/clock.js` stands in front of the game's scripts and makes it keep time by a clock that the recorder moves on by exactly one frame, and every frame is a screenshot of the page. `tools/trailer/shots.js` holds the shots as data: where each begins in the score's bars (a bar is three seconds, so every cut falls on one), how it opens a room or a cutscene (the way the playtest's direct stops do), what moves in it, the lines it speaks and the card under it. A line must be a line of the game, in English as a chapter has it: the game translates it as it shows it, and the recorder fails a shot whose line is not in the game's files or has no translation. During a shot the game says nothing else, and a scene shot keeps the game busy, so that nothing that waits for the player is drawn. The gameplay beat and the three puzzles (the counting board, the bolt of cloth and the table of scrolls) are played live: the recorder moves the real mouse and clicks as a player would (`ptr` in `shots.js`), and the trailer paints the game's own pointer where it is, since a screenshot shows none. A puzzle is worked one move or two, and never to its end; the table of scrolls is only wound back, because winding a scroll on past its last place finds Chapter Ten's hidden treasure. The cards say what the game is and name no church (CONTRIBUTING section 1), in both languages; "Thirteen chapters and a prologue" is counted from `CHAP`. A card that several shots in a row share fades in at the first and out at the last. The opening picture is where the page starts, so the title is filmed first and joined at the end. The trailer leaves out the light on the road to Damascus (a cut would make it flash), the hidden treasures and the answers to the puzzles, and its gameplay beat is in Chapter Ten, where the narration already calls him Paul. When chapters are added, give a new shot its bar in `shots.js` (the shots follow one another without a gap) and make the film again; the videos are not committed.
