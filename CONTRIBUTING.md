# Contributing

Paul of Tarsus is a Bible-based adventure meant to be suitable for Jehovah's Witness families. The rules below apply to everyone who changes it: the maintainer, contributors who propose changes, and AI coding assistants working in this repository. When a new rule is agreed, it is added to this file in the same change.

## 1. Scripture and history

### The Bible text

- Every Bible paraphrase and reference follows the **New World Translation of the Holy Scriptures (Study Edition)**, which you read on [jw.org](https://www.jw.org/en/library/bible/study-bible/books/) or in the [Watchtower Online Library](https://wol.jw.org/en/wol/binav/r1/lp-e/nwtsty).
- Look up every verse there before you write about it. Do not work from memory, and do not take what a verse means from another translation or from another edition of the New World Translation.
- Tell what a verse says in your own words, with its reference. Do not copy the Study Edition's wording into the game or its documentation (see "Terms of use of jw.org" below). A paraphrase follows the meaning of the New World Translation and must not say more or less than the verse does. Never invent a quotation, or present a paraphrase as one: a paraphrase stands without quotation marks, on the wax tablets too.
- A paraphrase is your own when it keeps no run of about six or more consecutive words of the Study Edition, and does not keep the verse's clauses in their order with only other words swapped in. Its meaning, its tense and its short distinctive terms ("undeserved kindness", "holy spirit", "the Grave", "loyal love") stay as the Study Edition has them. Do not get there by inverting the word order unnaturally ("Jesus it knew"); change the angle of the sentence instead: another subject, reported speech, the outcome or the purpose first. Nor by putting an object in front or wedging in "so they charged" or „sagte er“: a line that has to be read twice has gone wrong. A plain sentence that tells less beats a faithful one that has to be read twice: tell what happened as the narrator would, in one or two short sentences in natural word order, and leave out a minor clause rather than bend the sentence round it, as long as nothing is added and the meaning stays the verse's. Check each line against the verse before you commit it. A verse that is told in more than one chapter, such as a note on the wax tablets, has one wording in all of them.
- Other translations may be consulted for ideas on wording and sentence shape, never for the meaning: in English, Byington's *The Bible in Living English* and Rotherham's *Emphasised Bible*; in German, BIBEL.HEUTE and *Hoffnung für Alle*. Never copy any of them 1:1 either, and do not stitch a sentence together from their fragments. Write your own wording with your own sentence structure, shorter where possible.
- Use the divine name wherever the New World Translation has it in the passage: "Jehovah" (in German "Jehova"), never "the Lord", "der Herr" or "God" in its place. Where the New World Translation has "God" or "Lord", keep that.
- If you cannot check what a verse says, give the reference only, and list it for the maintainer to check.
- Give the reference with the line it belongs to, for example "(James 1:5)", or on the chapter's end screen.

### Historical information

- Check dates, places, routes, distances, people, offices, customs, trades, clothing, buildings and money against [jw.org](https://www.jw.org) or [wol.jw.org](https://wol.jw.org): the Study Edition's notes, media and maps, *Insight on the Scriptures*, and the other publications there.
- Where those sources are silent, a detail may be invented, but it must be plausible and must not contradict them. For example, the Scriptures say nothing of how the relief in Chapter Three was packed and counted, so those details are invented.
- If you cannot verify something, leave it out or ask the maintainer. In a pull request, list what you checked and where.

### Understanding and wording

- Follow the scriptural understanding of Jehovah's Witnesses. Use "Jehovah", "C.E." and "B.C.E.", "the congregation" and "the brothers".
- Fellow believers call one another brother and sister the way the Scriptures do: a name followed by the word, as when Ananias greets Saul (Acts 9:17), the word by itself (Acts 21:20), or our brother, our sister beside a name (1 Corinthians 1:1; Romans 16:1). Brother or Sister never stands before a name as a title, as in "Brother Paul" or „Bruder Paulus“: the first Christians did not use it so, in either language.
- Watch for details that are easy to get wrong. Acts 11:26 says the disciples were called Christians "by divine providence", so the name must not be presented as a nickname coined by outsiders.
- Use jw.org and wol.jw.org to check facts only. Do not copy their artwork, photographs, maps or publication text into the game.

### Terms of use of jw.org

The [Terms of Use](https://www.jw.org/en/terms-of-use/) of jw.org (which cover its subdomains, such as wol.jw.org) let you view, download and print its text, artwork, music, photos and videos for your own personal, non-commercial use, and share links to downloadable publications. They do not let you post that material or its trademarks on any website, distribute it with or as part of a software application, use it for a commercial purpose, or use it for marketing. This game is a software application and its repository is a website, so:

- Neither the game nor the documentation reproduces text from the Study Edition (verses, study notes, footnotes), from *Insight on the Scriptures* or from any other publication on jw.org or wol.jw.org: no quotation marks around their wording, and no close paraphrase that keeps a source sentence's structure and changes only a word here and there. A verse is told with its reference in wording and sentence structure of your own, as "The Bible text" above describes; for anything else, name the publication, chapter or article and say in your own words what it establishes.
- No artwork, photograph, map, music, video, logo or trademark of the Watch Tower Bible and Tract Society or of jw.org appears in the game, its icon, its screenshots or its documentation. Everything is painted and synthesized in code (section 4).
- The game and its pages are not marketed with the name, logo or authority of jw.org or of Jehovah's Witnesses. The README says that this is an independent, non-commercial project that they do not produce, endorse or review; keep that sentence, and keep the game free of charge and free of advertising.
- Do not build or ship a tool that collects, copies or scrapes data from jw.org or wol.jw.org. Look things up by hand.
- Check the Terms of Use again when you change anything in this section: they may be revised.

## 2. Story and puzzles

- Paul gets out of trouble with his trade, cooperation with fellow believers, honesty, practical wisdom and trust in Jehovah. No puzzle is solved by violence, deceit or a miracle used as a shortcut.
- Paul never lies. That includes every dialogue choice offered to the player: a wrong choice may be unwise, but never untrue.
- Where a line stands in a choice never tells which one is right. The engine (`choose`) shows the lines of every choice in a mixed order, ranked by their keys, so that it is the same each time and in every language, and when a line drops out the others keep their order. Only a menu of questions keeps the order it is written in, and a line that only leaves or only asks to hear more stays at the end; a line that is itself an answer, such as accepting an invitation, is mixed with the rest. A sequence, such as the lines of a psalm or the points of a speech, is mixed too, and where it can be, its parts stay on offer after they have been used, so that the next one is found by knowing it and not by counting down a list.
- Humour is light and kind: about one moment per room at most, and mostly where a curious player finds it, never in the serious beats of the main path. New jokes come from invented characters, from animals, and from Paul being modest about himself and his trade; companions named in the Scriptures may be warm and easy with one another, but nobody named in the Scriptures is the butt of a joke. There is none in narration of the account, a Bible line, a prayer hint, a tablet or an end screen, and none in a scene of suffering. Nothing is funny at the expense of Jehovah, Jesus, the holy spirit, prayer or the faith, and nobody is mocked for what he believes or for the people he belongs to. Paul's irony is literally true. German gets a joke of its own, never a translated pun. Every new joke is approved by the maintainer before it goes in.
- **Pray to Jehovah** is the hint system. It never changes the game's state. The first prayer at a point gives a gentle hint, and praying again gives a more direct one. Where the player plays someone who does not worship Jehovah, such as the governor's clerk of Chapter Twenty-One, the button reads "Think it over" („Nachdenken“) and gives the same hints as that person's own thought: no prayer is shown and none is counted.
- A puzzle may be timed by a slow beat, as the deck is crossed between two seas in Chapter Twenty-Two and as Paul keeps step with a soldier on the Appian Way in Chapter Twenty-Four (decided 2026-11-26). The beat is seen and heard from one clock (`shared/beat.js`), so that it can be read without sound, and it works by touch. Its sound is the beat's own, a sea or a footfall, and never a drum in the music, which runs on a clock of its own: where a beat is kept, the music has no drum beside it. A miss costs only the step and leaves no line to click away. Help grows by itself: after three misses someone calls the moment, and after three more he takes the player across, so that nobody can get stuck.
- Invented characters and all dialogue are dramatization. Each chapter's end screen says so, and the "About the content" section of the chapter's page in `docs/` names the invented characters. The last chapter has no end screen of its own: the game's closing screen takes its place, and the chapter says these things, and what the account leaves unsaid, on a page behind it, which one line on the closing screen opens (decided for issue #95, 2026-11-26).
- A person named in the Scriptures appears only in events where the account places that person, or where the Study Edition's notes or the publications on wol.jw.org say that the person was likely there. In the second case the chapter's end screen and the README say what the account itself leaves unsaid. For example, Acts 14 does not name the disciples at Lystra; the note on 2 Timothy 1:5 says that Lois and Eunice likely became believers during that stay, so Chapter Six shows them with Timothy, and says that Acts does not.
- The narration follows the account's own voice. It calls him Saul until Acts 13:9 and Paul from there on, and it says "we" where Acts does: from Troas in Chapter Eight (Acts 16:10), and "they" again where the account returns to it (Acts 16:19).
- The story of Acts ends where Acts ends: in Rome, inside the two years of Acts 28:30, 31, with the door open, and the narrator's roll is closed on it. After it the game ends more gladly, as the maintainer decided on 2026-11-26 (this replaces the decision of issue #95 that the release is never shown): under the caption "After Acts" one morning is played, the morning of Paul's release. Acts does not tell it, and the publications give it with "apparently", about 61 C.E. (the Study Edition's note on Acts 28:30), so the narrator says it with that word, and no court, no emperor and no verdict is seen. The soldier of the watch unlocks the chain, Paul takes his cloak and goes out alone into the street: nobody named in the Scriptures is placed at that morning, for no source puts anyone there. Then the map lights only the places that his later letters name (Titus 1:5; 3:12; 1 Timothy 1:3; 2 Timothy 4:13, 20), with no road between them, for no source gives their order, and Spain as a perhaps (Romans 15:24, 28; the publications say it is not known). The page behind the closing screen says what of this is invented. The second imprisonment and his death are not part of the game, and nothing in it mentions them.
- In Rome the game shows that the news of Paul's bonds went through the Praetorian Guard, and that believers of Caesar's household sent their greetings (Philippians 1:13; 4:22). It does not show Paul preaching to that household or winning it, and no soldier who guards him is shown becoming a believer or says that he believes: the letters do not say so (decided for issue #95, 2026-11-26).
- Before building a new chapter, agree on its episode with the maintainer by opening an issue.
- Every chapter hides one **hidden treasure**: a small thing its story does not need, such as a detail, a word or a sound that a curious player may come across. A new chapter is not finished without one. Finding it changes nothing in the game's state: no flag, no item, nothing in a safe-point code. It is not on the way through the chapter, and prayer never hints at it. Every rule of this file holds for it as for the rest of the chapter, and it never brings in a verse or a historical detail that has not been checked. The chapter names it and a point a little before it in its line of the `CHAP` manifest, calls `treasure()` where it is found, and adds the steps that find it to `TSTEPS` in the playtest.
- The game never calls them Easter eggs, in any language, and nothing in it refers to Easter: Jehovah's Witnesses do not keep it. They are **hidden treasures** ("verborgene Schätze").

## 3. Images and symbols

- No halos, crosses, mystical effects or pagan symbols. Where the account itself names a light from heaven, on the road to Damascus (Acts 9:3; 22:6, 11; 26:13), the screen goes plain white without rays, beams or figure; it comes up over at least four tenths of a second and never flashes.
- This includes cross shapes that appear by accident in pixel art. Watch for:
  - a tiny ellipse one pixel high, which rasterizes as a plus sign;
  - any small round shape with a radius under about one pixel of the view, such as the hole of a ring, a bead or the rim of a very small jar, which rasterizes as a plus sign too: draw it as a small square;
  - a vertical line over a horizontal band, such as rope lashings or yarn skeins;
  - grid lines or map lines that meet at right angles, and route lines on a map that cross each other;
  - a label or text laid across a thin line, such as a lake, river or coast;
  - "+" or "x" markers, and cross-shaped cursors or highlights;
  - four small squares set two by two, where the gap between them is a plus sign;
  - a rope or strap hanging straight down from the middle of a beam, which makes a T, and ties drawn across a roll or a bundle;
  - a tower that rises from the middle of a wall, or a post from the middle of a rail: set it at the end;
  - a sword with its guard. Where the account names a drawn sword (Acts 16:27), the hand covers the guard and the blade is one slanting line;
  - a mast or a post that stands across the level line where the sea meets the sky: keep the upright out of the picture, or the horizon behind something;
  - the joints of a bench or a wall drawn across its courses, and the threads of a loose weave drawn both ways: let each joint stop at the edge of its own course, and the threads run one way;
  - in a close view of a thing, such as a scroll or a bolt of cloth: a roller with a handle standing out of it or a tie round it, ruled lines that meet a margin, and four corners of laid-out pieces meeting in one point;
  - a column under a beam: paint a colonnade as a row of columns under a deep beam and a roof, give a column a capital hardly wider than its shaft, and lay no single block across two columns that stand free;
  - a statue with an arm stretched out, or with a spear held upright beside a shield: every arm is down, bent, or folded into the mantle;
  - the legs of a walking figure crossing below the hem as an X: the hips come together as it walks, so that the legs open like a pair of compasses; a foot that reaches across the foot of a spear, which makes a T upside down: whoever carries a spear steps short; and an arm that swings across the body as one dark slanting bar: the near arm swings forward only to the edge of the body;
  - a living figure with an arm stretched out to the side, or with both arms raised: when a hand moves, the forearm is bent and slanting, only the far hand is raised, and whoever holds a spear keeps his hand on it;
  - a tool hung by its handle with a blade or a head across it, such as a half-moon knife on a wall; a plank, an oar or a strap laid across a hull; a spar lying across the props of a ship; a bench with a leg under its middle; a board, a shelf or a table that runs behind a row of columns, each of which crosses it; and iron bands laid across the planks of a door.
  - a block on a post that is thinner than it, such as a sundial on its pier: make the pier as wide as the block; and the stairs of a theater drawn across its tiers: leave them out, and let a walkway follow the curve of the seats.
  - a balance whose beam hangs from one upright, or has a pointer standing at its middle: hang the beam from two cords that part as they rise, leave the pointer out, and let each pan hang by cords that meet at the end of the beam; a lamp that is wider than the stand under it: give the stand a top as wide as the lamp, and a foot that spreads; and a hanging lamp on one cord from the middle of a beam: hang it by cords that part upward.
  - on a lane map seen from above: two lanes that cross, two that come to a third from opposite sides at the same place, or four ways that meet at one point, each of which is a plus sign; and four roofs whose corners meet at one point. Lanes meet as forks and bends, or offset along the street they come to; the playtest checks all of these (`lanesall`). Ephesus is an accepted exception, for the sake of accuracy: its lower city was laid out as a grid of streets at right angles, so on its map the streets cross (`grid:true`). The mark where a walk ends is a small hollow diamond, never a cross or four corner brackets, and seen from the front no stripe runs down the middle of a figure's mantle, which a hand before it would cross.
- A halo can come about by accident too: a round pool, a round window, a shield or a lamp's glow that lies just where a head comes to stand reads as a ring round it. In a room where people walk, look at what lies behind their heads along the whole strip, and give such a thing a shape that is long and low, or move it.
- A tree is painted as it grows. A conifer is never a stack of triangles: that is the picture of a Christmas tree, and Jehovah's Witnesses do not keep Christmas. The engine's `pine` paints every pine this way: a trunk that leans out of the rock, boughs that climb, and needles in ragged clumps.
- A letter in an inscription is a letter, not a cross shape: Greek capitals cut in stone may include tau and chi. Each inscription is still looked at enlarged. The same holds for Greek letters that stand for numbers, as on the counting board of Chapter Thirteen: the letters used are the correct ones for the period, and one that is shaped like a cross is an accepted exception, for the sake of realism. So it is with the Roman number letters cut into the milestones of the Appian Way in Chapter Twenty-Four (I, V, X and L): an X on a milestone is a letter (decided 2026-11-26).
- Where the account itself names something used in false worship, it may be shown plainly when the scene is about refusing it: the bulls and garlands of Acts 14:13 are shown at the gate of Lystra.
- A temple, an altar, a statue or an image of a god is not shown, with one exception, made for historical accuracy: in **Athens, Corinth, Ephesus and Rome** pagan temples, altars and deities may be displayed, because those cities cannot be shown as they were without them. Athens is Chapter Eleven, where the account itself turns on what Paul saw (Acts 17:16, 23, 24, 29); Corinth is Chapter Twelve, where two temples are seen from afar. Rome is Chapters Twenty-Four and Twenty-Five: two temple fronts in the haze where the city is seen from the Appian Way, none at its gate, and one far off through the window of Paul's lodging. Nothing is painted that did not yet stand in Paul's day: no Colosseum, no arch of Titus, no great dome. Wherever the exception is used:
  - What is shown is recognizable and restrained: a temple from outside, an altar as an inscribed block, a statue as a draped figure. Nothing is unclothed, no image of a god is seen in close-up, and what the publications describe as indecent is left out altogether (the pillars at the entrance to the marketplace of Athens, for one).
  - No rite is shown: no sacrifice, fire, smoke or offering on an altar, no procession, and nobody praying or bowing to an image.
  - A god is named on the screen only where the scene needs it.
  - Paul never admires one, and the player never uses one as a tool, a key or a hiding place. The one use the game makes of any of them is the one Paul made of the altar in Acts 17:23.
  - Every other rule of this section holds for them.
- Jehovah's temple in Jerusalem is drawn as it was, for historical accuracy (decided for Chapter Eighteen, 2026-11-25): the Court of the Gentiles with its colonnades, the barrier with its warning stones, the gates and inner courts, and the sanctuary seen from the courts. Its layout and proportions are checked against the Study Edition's media and the publications on wol.jw.org, and painted in our own way: nothing of their artwork is copied (section 1). No sacrifice, fire, smoke or rite is shown on its altar, and no Roman standard is seen in the city.
- A Roman military standard and a ship's figurehead are images too, and the publications call the standards objects of worship (*Insight on the Scriptures*, "Army"; the Study Edition's note on Matthew 24:15). They are shown in two places, for historical accuracy (decided 2026-11-26): the standards of the garrison in the audience chamber at Caesarea (Chapter Twenty-One, Acts 25:23), and the figurehead of the ship of Acts 28:11 (Chapter Twenty-Four). Wherever this exception is used:
  - A standard stands at the back of the picture, small, and is never seen in close view. What it carries cannot be made out.
  - Nobody honors or salutes a standard, nobody speaks of one, Paul never looks at one with interest, and the player never looks at, uses or touches one.
  - A standard is a pole with discs, drawn as small squares. It has no crossbar and no cloth hanging from one, which would make a T, and its pole crosses no band or course of the wall behind it.
  - The figurehead is a small painted board on the side of the ship's bow, laid along her planks: one draped figure too small to make out, and under it a name in Greek capitals (decided by mockup, 2026-11-26). *Insight on the Scriptures* ("Zeus, Sons of") supposes an image or symbol with the name of one twin on each side of the bow; no source shows one. It is seen once, on the ship at the quay of Malta, at her own size in the picture, and never in a close view. It is no hotspot of its own: the ship's look line says that she carries the sign. Because the account itself names it (Acts 28:11), Luke asks what it is and writes it down, and a sailor says in one plain line what sailors hoped for from the twins. Nobody honors it, Paul says nothing of it, and the player does nothing with it.
  - Every other rule of this section holds for them, and no standard is seen in Jerusalem.
- Three things of the grain ship of Acts 27 are shown as they were, for historical accuracy (decided for Chapters Twenty-Two and Twenty-Three, 2026-11-26), though this section lists their shapes:
  - An anchor of that time, a wooden shank with a heavy stock of lead across its head: on the ship at Myra, hanging at her bow where she is seen from the side, and lying on her deck. Nowhere else: where anchors are let go or cut away (Acts 27:29, 40), only their cables are seen.
  - The ropes that undergird her (Acts 27:17): round her hull where she is seen from the side in the storm and as a wreck. They slant and curve with the belly of the hull, and are never straight bars square to its planks.
  - Her sternpost ends in a swan's neck and head that looks the way she sails, as the publications draw such a ship. It is a carved ornament of plain wood and not a figurehead: nothing is set under it, nobody looks to it or speaks of it, and it is never seen in close view. Its loop must not stand as a ring round anyone's head.
  - Every other rule of this section holds for the ship: her masts and yards, her oars, her rail, her lantern, her cables and her wreckage.
- Two shapes are accepted as they are (decided 2026-11-26, issue #98): the end of a figure's belt, which hangs straight down from the buckle at the middle of the belt, and a joist of a beamed ceiling that meets the wall plate upright where we look straight along it. Neither is treated as a cross shape, and neither is changed in passing.
- Look at screenshots of every changed scene before committing.

## 4. Look and sound

- Backdrops are painted in the manner of early-1990s VGA adventure games: painterly and detailed, with depth and real lighting. The scenes are our own. Never copy another game's art, characters or music.
- Characters are pixel-placed sprites drawn to the same standard as the backdrops: three-quarter view, crisp hand-placed pixels, flat bands of light and shadow, no outline, and lit by the room they stand in. Dress and head coverings fit the period, the place and the person.
- A figure has the proportions of the sprites of those games, not of life: it stands about five heads tall, with a head large enough for its face to be read on a small screen. Every head is drawn by hand at its own size, never as an enlarged copy of a smaller one. After a change to the figure painter or to a head, look at the whole cast, enlarged: `node tools/playtest.mjs cast`.
- In a Greek city the men of standing wear the Greek outer garment: a rectangular length of cloth wrapped over a tunic. The game's text calls it a mantle, not a toga, which the publications keep for a Roman garment. Greek women look as they did in the first century: a tunic to the feet, the mantle over it, and the hair parted and bound up, or the mantle drawn over the head. No woman is shown on the Areopagus: Damaris (Acts 17:34) listens from the path below it.
- A scene has depth. Whoever stands farther back is drawn behind: a man who walks behind the carrier's mule is hidden by it, and hides it when he stands in front. This holds for everything that stands inside a room's walkable strip, whether it is a person, an animal, or a thing painted into the backdrop such as a stool, a low table or a heap of sailcloth. In a new room, walk behind and in front of everything that stands out on the floor, and look.
- Whoever sits rests on the seat. Every seated figure sits the same way: the hips on the seat, the thighs level, the near shin straight down to a foot set flat on the floor, and the far foot drawn back under the seat, so that the shins open and never cross. A short tunic leaves the knees bare beyond its hem, and a long robe hangs from the knees nearly to the floor. Whoever is bound keeps his feet side by side, and whoever sits on a ledge above the floor, as Eutychus in the window, lets his legs hang before it. The cloth at the hips rests exactly on the top edge of the seat: the seat is made as high as the pose needs, or the sitter is set at the seat's own foot line, and a figure is never lifted or sunk onto a seat. Look at every seat enlarged with someone on it (decided 2026-11-25).
- Nobody walks through what stands on the floor. Whatever stands inside a room's walkable strip, a stool, a table, a goat, a boat drawn up on the sand, has a footprint, the floor it covers (`foot` beside its entry in `props`, or `ROOMS[id].block` for what the room paints itself), and every walk goes round it: the player's and the scripts' alike. So does everyone who is standing still when the walk begins; two who are both walking may pass each other. Only a seat (`seat:1`) may be walked into, by a script that sits someone on it. A click on a place that cannot be reached walks as near to it as the floor allows. In a new room, run `node tools/playtest.mjs routes<chapter>` and look at its sheets: the footprints lie where the things meet the floor, and no footprint closes off an exit or a hotspot (issue #83, decided 2026-11-24).
- A scene has one horizon. Its sea, its fields and its paving run back to one level, about as high as the eyes of the people who stand in it, and whatever is seen through a gate, a window or a colonnade ends at that level too: the country behind a city gate has no lower horizon of its own. The engine's `gateView` paints that country, and each gate gives it the horizon and the sky of its own scene. Where an opening shows a street, either a house front closes the view or the street runs back to that same level. In a new room, lay a level line across the picture and look. One room is an accepted exception (decided 2026-11-26): Rome seen from afar in Chapter Twenty-Four, where the road comes down from the hills and the far land ends above the walkers' heads, as a view from a rise.
- A round thing that lies on its side shows one end. Of a roll or a bale of cloth, a log or a jar on its side we see the end that is turned to us, as a face, and never the other: the far end is only the round outline of the thing, in the tones of its side, and what runs round it bends the way that outline does. The engine's `clothRoll` paints every roll and bale of cloth this way. Look at such a thing enlarged.
- Water runs somewhere. A river or a channel has no end that stops short in the land: it comes out of the distance as a thread, widens as it nears, and its mouth lies in the sea or in a basin. Where it meets a shore, the line of the shore opens for it, and nothing grows in it. Look at both ends enlarged.
- What stands on a sloping line stands on it. The battlements of a wall that runs away from us are blocks whose foot follows the slope of the wall's top: an upright block set on a slope leaves a wedge of sky under its far corner. Look at such a wall enlarged.
- Close views follow the finer painterly standard of Chapters Thirteen and Fourteen (such as the sundial and the sealed tablet): fine-grained wood that never competes with incised letters or captions, veined and weathered stone, horizontal papyrus fibers, and soft contact shadows, rather than heavy high-contrast wood overlays or flat fills. A wooden ship's hull is painted in curved plank strakes with staggered butt joints stopping inside each course, never with flat floorboard texture. A ship moored behind a quay, together with its steering oars, rises and falls behind the stone edge of the quay and any mooring posts on it, never in front of them; only the gangplank reaches over the quay edge onto the paving.
- Inventory icons keep the dark wood panel unchanged; dark cloth, leather and wood items lift their midtones and carry a crisp rim sheen (`IH.roll` sets a top highlight) so they remain legible against the slots even when dimmed.
- A lamp set in a recessed wall niche casts its light outward and downward from the opening (`nicheLight`), so the arch above occludes upward spill instead of throwing a bright circular halo onto the wall above it. Rain, wet paving and capillary damp along wall bases use the shared weather helper (`shared/weather.js`: short wind-slanted strokes, layered puddles and ripples).
- Every figure that stands on the floor gets a soft contact shadow under its feet and, in rooms whose `light` names lamps, braziers or directional sun/moonlight, up to two softened `#181220` `multiply` silhouette shadows cast away from the strongest lights. Where a lamp stands out in front of a figure (`lz > y`), the shadow runs back across the floor to the foot of the wall (`wallY`) and bends upright onto the solid spans of the back wall (`wall`), stopping cleanly at open doorways, arches, windows and sky. All figure shadows are drawn immediately after the backdrop and the room's floor painting, underneath every actor and every `props` cutout, so no shadow ever paints over a character or a foreground prop; in pitch dark (`cell9` before the earthquake) no shadow is drawn, and a figure perched high above the floor (such as in a window) casts none. Look at changed scenes and `cast-shadows.png` (`node tools/playtest.mjs shadows`) to verify clean wall creases and make sure no projected limb or spear crosses a floor joint as a plus sign, X or T (section 3).
- Every room where people stand says what light it has (`ART[id].light`), and a room whose light comes from one side, the sun, the moon, a lamp or a brazier, gives its figures cast shadows that fall the way its painted light falls: the same side as the shadows of its walls and things, long in the morning and evening, short at midday. `sun:[dx, dy, strength]` puts the tip of a shadow `dx, dy` room pixels from the feet of a figure 46 pixels tall. Only soft light, an overcast sky, deep shade or a night without a lamp, keeps the contact shadow alone (`amb` only, with a word in the code saying why), and a close view or a map that nobody stands in says `light:null`. After a change, look at the sheets of `node tools/playtest.mjs shadows<chapter>`; `shadows` fails on a room that says nothing about its light and on a sun too short to cast a shadow (decided 2026-11-25).
- Figures move when they speak, and the movement is drawn to the same standard: every frame of a mouth, a chin, a head or a hand is placed by hand in the figure painter, never made by scaling or turning another frame. After adding or changing one, look at all of them enlarged on `cast-talk.png` (`node tools/playtest.mjs cast`), for a chin or a beard that has come apart and for cross shapes; the playtest fails if a pose is in more than one piece. A figure that works with a tool in its hand, such as a hammer at an anvil, moves its arm with the tool through its frames and never leaves the arm hanging still while the tool moves. A figure that bows keeps its head still. A statue never moves.
- A walk follows the ground and never a clock: the frame comes from the distance covered, so that a foot that stands is not carried along, a step is heard as a foot lands, and a walk begins and ends on a shorter step. Everybody walks at the same speed, so that companions stay together; what differs from one person to the next is the step. Every frame of a stride is worked out by the figure painter for every look; after a change, look at them enlarged on `cast-walk.png` (`node tools/playtest.mjs cast`).
- A lane map (`shared/lanes.js`) shows a city at a slant from above, as the city maps of those games did: every house stands up from its ground, its front wall of one or two storeys towards us with its door and windows, and its roof above, so that it hides the lane behind it; while whoever is played walks behind a house it fades, so that he is never lost from sight. The people on it are figures of their own, about fourteen room pixels tall, drawn by hand pixel by pixel like the room figures, with a head large enough to tell which way they face and no face to read. Their walk follows the ground as in the rooms. Where the story has people hurry, as a runner with a message and a crowd that rushes to the theater, they run, all at one pace of their own, and whoever walks keeps the walking pace: on the map Paul walks. A crowd comes from somewhere: in by a gate or along a street from beyond the map, or out of the door of a house, which stands open behind him for a moment; nobody appears out of the open ground, and nobody comes out of a house whose shutters are bolted. Where a narrow street is jammed, the people in it stand packed and cannot get through themselves, and nobody passes them. Whatever blocks a way, people, a barred gate or an overturned stall, is named: whoever is played says what it is when he stops before it (`obstacles`, and the `say` of a jam). A click walks along the lanes, shown as a row of dots, by the lanes he knows: those he has seen on the screen where no house hides them, and those round every corner he has come by, so that the way through a maze is found by the player and not by the path-finder. The player may zoom the map with two fingers, the mouse wheel, or + and -. Every pose of the map's figures is one piece (`node tools/playtest.mjs lanesfolk` shows them all enlarged).
- A movement says something, and what it says must be true. A nod is a yes and a shake of the head is a no: the engine never gives either by itself, so that nobody, and Paul least of all, seems to agree with what he does not agree with. Whoever is spoken to may raise his brows or blink, and no more. A script directs a nod, a shake of the head or a hand on the chest, or the chapter lists the line in its `ACTING` table: only where the line itself plainly says so, and never on a thought.
- Two who talk look at each other, and neither hides the other. Whoever speaks faces the one he speaks to, and that one faces him, for as long as the scene lasts; the engine turns them (`say`), and when Paul walks up to someone he stands beside that person, where nobody else is standing. A script names the one spoken to wherever the engine cannot know it: when two other people talk with each other, when a line is meant for someone other than the character the player controls, and when it is meant for nobody in particular, such as a crowd (`to:null`). A script that places someone for a talk sets him beside the other, never on the same spot, and never just in front of him or behind him. The playtest fails on a line whose speaker or listener is turned away or hidden.
- Narration is never set on a bare black screen. A line told while the picture is dark is read from the narrator's scroll (`shared/scroll.js`): an open roll of papyrus, held in two hands by lamplight. A chapter does nothing for it but fade out, or set the scene `DARK`, and tell. A roll and not a single sheet, because what the narrator tells is for the most part Luke's account, a book. The hands follow the maintainer's example: seen from the back, the thumb resting on its roll with its nail and the folds of its joint, in a short scale of skin tones with the backdrops' own dither. In that picture only the two flames move, with the small glow round each: nothing brightens and darkens the whole of it. There is one exception, for the end of the game (decided for issue #95, 2026-11-26): after the narrator's last line the reader's hands close the roll. Both hands roll their ends in to the middle, as a long roll with a stick at each end was rolled up (*Insight on the Scriptures*, "Scroll, Roll"), and the closed roll is held between the two lamps. Every frame of the hands is placed by hand; the frames follow each other as plain cuts, in about three seconds, with no blending; the flames go on moving; and the open roll is not changed by it. After a change, look at every frame enlarged (`node tools/playtest.mjs proll`, and the frames of `SHARED.scroll.picture`). The one place where narration stands on a plain screen without the scroll is the words told while the screen is white on the road to Damascus, set in dark ink because the narrator's room is by lamplight.
- Animals are drawn to the same standard as the people. Every part grows out of the one before it: a tail is set on at the rump, a neck comes out of the shoulders in the body's own tones, a head sits on its neck, and nothing floats free. Two animals that stand together must not overlap so that the head of one seems to belong to the body of the other. Look at them enlarged, in every walking frame and facing both ways: `node tools/playtest.mjs beasts`, for the mule `node tools/playtest.mjs mules`, for the ox `node tools/playtest.mjs oxen`, and for the sheep `node tools/playtest.mjs sheep`. The playtest fails if a sprite is in more than one piece.
- The open air has its birds (`shared/birds.js`). Every harbor scene has gulls wheeling over the water, and a gull or two on the quay where it has an edge. Over open country with no buildings a bird of prey circles high up, swallows skim a river, a lark climbs over the fields, and in spring a line of storks passes north. On the roofs and walls of a town sit sparrows and doves, which fly up when whoever is played comes under them and come back after he has gone. Birds are seen only by day and in fair weather: at night and in rain or a storm none is in the air, and in the rain gulls sit hunched on the quay. None is shown in a scene of suffering. A bird in flight is a bent line of pixels or a shallow V, never a level stroke with a body or a tail across it, a sitting bird shows no legs, and every flight stays in open sky, clear of masts, stays, towers and roofs. Each kind can be looked at, from where one stands. Birds change nothing in the story. After a change, look at `birds-sheet.png` (`node tools/playtest.mjs birds`) (decided 2026-11-24).
- The game opens on its opening picture (`shared/splash.js`): its name over a tentmaker's bench by lamplight, once each time the game is opened and not when the player comes back to the title from a game. It is silent until the player clicks or presses a key, and never tries to sound before that. The click starts the overture; when its melody has been heard, or on a second click, the picture passes into the title's own and the chapters appear. In that picture only the flame moves. The game closes on the same bench by day, behind its closing screen (`SHARED.splash.day`, decided for issue #95, 2026-11-26): a second painting of the same things, the lamp out, more rolls on the bench than at night, in plain daylight from a window that is not seen, with no rays, beams or shafts, the sun lying low on the bench and the foot of the wall, and every shadow falling away from the window. Nothing in it moves, and the picture by lamplight is not changed by it. The closing screen's words stand on the upper wall, each line inside a course of the stone, and the bench stays clear.
- On a phone held sideways the screen is laid out for the phone (`fit` and `lensTick` in `index.html`): the verbs stand in a column on the left, the inventory in a column on the right, and the field between them is always filled by the picture from top to bottom, cropped at the sides. It follows whoever is played, or whoever speaks, moves under one finger and zooms with two, up to three times. A close view and the map are fitted into the field whole, with timber above and below them, so that no part of a puzzle is out of sight. The options, a safe point, an end screen and the narrator's scroll take the whole screen while the columns slide out. Held upright, a phone asks to be turned. The page itself is never zoomed. The player may choose the classic layout in the options. After a change to the layout, look at the phone stops (`node tools/playtest.mjs phoneall`, and its pictures) and at the game on a phone or in the iPhone Simulator (decided 2026-11-24).
- There are no image or audio files. Everything is painted in code on a canvas, and all music and sound are synthesized with the Web Audio API.
- Each chapter has its own score, and each room has a mood and ambience to match.
- The game ends with a finale (`shared/finale.js`): a map on which Paul does not move, the last line of Luke's account and the roll closed, the morning after Acts (the one part that is played, by the last chapter in its own rooms), the map of the places of his later letters with the narrator's last line, a look back over every journey, and the closing screen. In the look back each journey has a map of its own, so that no two roads cross (`shared/lookback.js`), with one narrated line that stands clear of the places on its roads; as a chapter's road is drawn, its name stands in the status line and one bar of its own tune is heard (`shared/tunes.js`, a copy that the stop `tunes` holds to the chapter's score: when a chapter's music changes, its bar is taken again). No tense music is heard anywhere in the finale. A click passes over what is playing, SKIP over everything that is shown up to the morning after Acts and, after it, up to the closing screen; the morning itself is played and has no SKIP. The title shows the look back and the closing screen again to whoever has finished (decided for issue #95, 2026-11-26; the morning after Acts added 2026-11-26).
- On the deck in the storm the ship is seen to move with the sea (decided 2026-11-26): the room sways slowly with the beat, a few degrees at most (`ART[id].sway`), with everything and everyone on her, while the sea, the sky, the rain and the spray do not. Nothing sways for a player whose device asks for reduced motion, or who has chosen calm movement in the options.
- The storm of Acts 27 has lightning (decided 2026-11-26), though the account names none, and the chapter's end screen says so. It stands far off in the sky and never lights the whole picture: the cloud near a stroke brightens and fades over at least four tenths of a second, never twice within three seconds, and the screen never flashes. A stroke is one thin broken line that runs downward; its branches part from it and never cross it, one another, a mast or a rope. There is none by day in fair weather, and none in the bay at daybreak.
- Tension is never a held drone. Where a scene is tense, the music moves, in the chapter's own key, bar and tune: a low string plucks the keynote and leans on the note above it, a drum ticks with it and almost stops every fourth bar, and a ney begins the chapter's tune and breaks off before it comes home. A tense mood is no louder than the chapter's other music. A low held note may lie quietly under a calm or a night scene (decided for issue #80, 2026-11-24).
- Sound follows the player's device. On iPhone and iPad the game stays quiet in Silent Mode; never work around it (no HTML audio element, no `playback` audio session). The game may say that Silent Mode could be on, but it must not claim to know: Safari does not tell a page.

## 5. Code

- `index.html` is the engine. Each chapter is one file, `levels/chapterN.js`, loaded only when that chapter is played. It holds the chapter's music, artwork, cast, items, rooms, dialog, cutscenes and prayer hints, with a key wherever a line is shown; the lines themselves are in `lang/en/chapterN.js` and in each other language's `chapterN.js` (section 8). A new chapter adds its file, its text files and one line to the `CHAP` manifest. Anything shared between chapters goes in its own file, also loaded on demand, not in a chapter file or the engine.
- The game must keep running when `index.html` is opened straight from disk: no build step, no server and no dependencies.
- The game is also an installable app that plays offline (`sw.js`, `shared/pwa.js`). It loads its service worker only over http(s), so it still runs from disk. Its icon and manifest are made in code like everything else: no icon or manifest file. A new file in `shared/` goes on the offline list in `shared/pwa.js`; `node tools/playtest.mjs pwa` checks it.
- Safe-point codes are a promise to players. Every code that works today must keep working. The browser's autosave (`localStorage`, key `paul.save`) is that same code and nothing else, so the promise covers it: never keep other state there without a format the decoder can tell apart from the code. If a change needs more state, add a new code format that the decoder can tell apart from the old one. Never reorder a chapter's `flags`, `rooms` or `share`; only append to them, within the widths the code format reserves. Player options (volumes, text speed) live in their own key (`paul.opts`), separate from `paul.save`. Saved games (the farthest point, the last point's time and the four slots) live in `paul.saves`, and every entry in it is a safe-point code, so the same promise covers each one. The hidden treasures found live in `paul.treasures`, apart from every saved game, as a treasure code and nothing else; it never stands in `paul.save` or `paul.saves`, and the decoder must always tell it from a safe-point code. Its format is a promise too: every treasure code that works today must keep working, a chapter keeps its bit, and what has been found is only ever added to, never lost. The farthest point is never overwritten by a point that is not farther, and only the player deletes it.
- That the game has been played to its end is kept as a safe-point code and nothing else (decided for issue #95, 2026-11-26): the last chapter's last safe point, the morning of its last scene, with one more flag set, which the finale sets as the closing screen comes. The chapter's line of the `CHAP` manifest says where that flag stands among the chapter's flags (`done`), so that the title can tell a finished game without loading the level. Continue leads to that point like any other. No time played and no count of prayers is kept or shown at the end: the game knows them for one sitting only, and there is no place for them in a code.
- A choice may be carried from one chapter into the next, and only in the safe-point code (decided for Chapters Twenty-Two and Twenty-Three, 2026-11-26). The next chapter's first flags hold it, and its line of the `CHAP` manifest says how many (`carry`). The end screen prints the next chapter's first code with those flags set, and its click starts the chapter with the same flags, so that the printed code and the click lead to the same story. A chapter started from the title has nothing carried and plays a default that agrees with the account. Nothing is ever read from another chapter's saved game.

## 8. Languages

The game is written in English and plays in German too. Every language is data under `lang/`, one folder per language, English as well: the code holds a key where a line is shown (`say(P,'cricket_somewhere_in_the_wall')`), `lang/en/` holds the English line for that key, and every other language holds its own text for the same key.

- New or changed player-visible text must be added in every supported language in the same change. A new line gets a key that says what the line is, in lowercase words joined by `_`, written once and never renamed, even when its English changes later; a key is unique in the game, and the same English line has the same key in every part. In every language but English an entry is `[text, fingerprint]`, where the fingerprint is that of the English line it was made from. When an English line changes, its key stays and every translation of it becomes stale: check each one against the new English, rewrite it if it no longer says the same, and record it with `node tools/strings.mjs --confirm <lang> <part> <key>` (`--show <lang> <part> --stale` lists them). The playtest fails on a line that has no text in a language, on a translation whose English line is gone, on a stale translation, and on a key that reaches the screen without its text.
- Every rule in section 1 applies in each language. German verses are read in the German New World Translation, Study Edition ("Neue-Welt-Übersetzung der Heiligen Schrift – Studienausgabe"), on [wol.jw.org](https://wol.jw.org/de/wol/binav/r10/lp-x/nwtsty) or [jw.org/de](https://www.jw.org/de/bibliothek/bibel/studienbibel/buecher/). Look up each verse there before you write about it, and tell it in your own German words with the reference; the terms of use in section 1 apply to the German text just as they do to the English. Never base a German line on your own translation of the English verse: check what the German Study Edition says, and write the German paraphrase from that. For wording ideas BIBEL.HEUTE and *Hoffnung für Alle* may be consulted as section 1 allows, and never copied; the divine name is "Jehova" wherever the German Study Edition has it. If you cannot check a verse, give the reference only and list it for the maintainer.
- References use the language's own book names and style, such as "(Apostelgeschichte 9:25)" or "(5. Mose 21:23)". Names of people and places follow that language's Study Edition, without the marks that show stress: Saulus, Paulus, Kephas, Jakobus, Stephanus, Damaskus, Cäsarea, Antiochia. Invented characters with a Bible name take its spelling there too (Noomi, Isai, Sakkai).
- In German, use the terms Jehovah's Witnesses use: "Jehova", "u. Z." and "v. u. Z.", "die Versammlung", "die Brüder", "unverdiente Güte". Acts 11:26 says the disciples were "durch göttliche Vorsehung" called Christians. The characters say "du" to one another, as people do in the German Bible. German text uses „ and “ for quotation marks.
- A translation says what the English line says, no more and no less. Paul never lies in any language, and the dialogue choices stand in the same order in every language (section 2), so every walkthrough holds in every language.
- The text is today's language for players under 30, in both languages (decided after the readability review of 2026-11-21):
  - English dialogue, look lines, hints and the UI contract as people speak ("don't", "it's", "I'll", "let's"). Full forms stay in the narration of the account, in Bible lines, on the tablets and the end screens, for officials at formal moments, and where the stress falls on "not" or on the verb. No "shall" (a frightened official's "It shall be done" may stay), no "whom" in speech, American usage ("around", "Do you have…?", "I'd like", "Keep it with you"), and no Bible English in invented dialogue ("make ready", "yonder", "need not", "in bonds").
  - German speech tells the past in the Perfekt; the Präteritum stays for sein, haben, the modal verbs, denken and wissen, in short „als“ clauses, and in all narration. No old Konjunktiv forms such as „würben“ or „stünde“ (say „würde …“), no Heische-Konjunktiv („dann sage er“: „dann soll er … sagen“), no „jener“, „von Jesus“ rather than „Jesu“, „Kapitän“ rather than „Schiffsherr“, „Treppe“ rather than „Stiege“, „Mantel“ for the outer garment of people (a Bible line keeps the Study Edition's own word), and a German turn of phrase rather than the English one translated word for word.
  - A form of address such as "sir" / „Herr“ stands at most once in a conversation; young people use the name or nothing.
  - A period word a young player may not know (span, gusset, cubit, fuller, threshing floor) stays, and one look line says in a few words what it is.
  - The second, more direct prayer hint is worded as a person speaks, and still names both things ("I should test the river with my staff").
- In code, write a key where player-visible text goes, never the text itself, and let the engine look it up as it is shown (`say`, `choose`, names, the tablets, the map). A line put together from parts is a template, such as `T('it_still_needs_list', {list})` with the English "It still needs {list}.", so that each language can word it in its own order; a choice of such lines gives `choose` keys to mix them by (`by`), or the order would differ from one language to the next. Never let the story's logic compare text: it compares keys.
- Names in the status line ("Use awl with torn awning") stand without an article. In a language with cases, choose names that read correctly after every verb, such as German compounds ("Vorratskorb (verstärkt)", not "verstärkter Korb").
- A speaking figure's mouth follows the words that stand on the screen, read as they are shown in the player's language: a level gives no data for it, and a translation needs none. Never time a mouth, a head or a hand by the English text of a line that is shown in another language. Where the narrator tells what a figure is saying, as with Tertullus in Chapter Twenty (Acts 24:2-8), that figure's mouth may move with the narrator's words (accepted 2026-11-26).
- A line that is sung is written as its notes, syllable by syllable, each with the vowel of its syllable. The singer's mouth follows those notes. The vowels and the rhythm follow the words on the screen, so they are language data: each language gives its own under the same key, as it gives the line itself, and the playtest checks that it does. A cry that the account puts in a crowd's mouth, such as the cry for Artemis of Acts 19:28 and 34, is heard the same way and never written on the screen: the vowels and the rhythm of the words the account gives, in the player's language, under one key (decided for Chapter Fourteen, 2026-11-22).
- Check every changed scene in each language: German is longer than English, and text must not overflow its box or cross a line on the map.
